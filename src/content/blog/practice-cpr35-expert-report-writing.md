## The report's two columns

Every radiological expert report I write separates, cleanly, what is **fact**
observed in the images from what is **opinion** about their meaning. A finding
is fact; a causal conclusion drawn from it is opinion. Conflating the two is the
most common reason an expert's credibility suffers in cross-examination.

## Structure I recommend

A pragmatic skeleton that serves claimants and defendants alike:

1. **Instructions and material** — the DICOM, reports and correspondence reviewed.
2. **Summary of opinion** — a short, direct statement up front.
3. **Imaging review** — technical protocol, findings by modality, in plain language.
4. **Assumptions and limitations** — including identifiers removed and the platform used.
5. **Responses to the questions posed** — each question answered directly.
6. **Basis paragraph** — what would or could change the opinion.

## Consistency duties

An expert's opinion must be consistent with the evidence, with the expert's own
published and clinical practice, and — importantly for a radiologist — with the
limits of what imaging can and cannot determine. It is legitimate to state that
a causal question falls outside radiology; that honesty is itself a duty under
the EMA Code of Practice.
