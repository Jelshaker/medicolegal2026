## **R (Smith) v Castle Point Borough Council**

**[2019] EWHC 2019 (Admin)**  
**High Court, Queen's Bench Division, Planning Court**  
**Mr C M G Ockelton, Vice President of the Upper Tribunal**  
**Judgment delivered: 31 July 2019**

<br>

### What was this case about?

This was a planning law judicial review challenging the grant of planning permission for a five-metre boundary wall surrounding a scrapyard. The claimant owned adjoining land and argued that the authority had failed to consider contamination risks, the potential for intensified use, and the impact on the Green Belt.

The court dismissed the challenge. It held that contamination assessments are not required for every development on known contaminated land, that a boundary wall does not intensify an existing use, and that separate environmental licensing regimes regulate contamination risks rather than the planning system.

### Why is this relevant to medicolegal law?

At first sight this is a planning case, not a clinical negligence case. However, it establishes principles that have direct medicolegal significance, particularly in relation to **contamination exposure claims, regulatory overlap, and the limits of duty of care**.

#### 1. Contamination and health injury claims

Contaminated land can be the subject of clinical negligence-type claims where harm results. A resident or worker who develops illness from exposure to toxic substances on a contaminated site may bring a personal injury claim grounded in negligence.

The medicolegal issues in such cases mirror those in clinical negligence:

- **Duty of care.** Does the party responsible for the site owe a duty to the claimant? This is generally established where proximity and foreseeable harm exist.
- **Breach.** Did the defendant fail to meet the standard of care? Expert evidence is required to establish what measures a reasonably responsible person or organisation would have taken.
- **Causation.** Did the contamination on the site probably cause the claimant's injury? Epidemiological, toxicological and medical evidence is essential.
- **Damage.** What was the extent of the harm suffered?

*Smith* is relevant because the court held that planning authorities are not required to assess contamination simply because a site is known to be contaminated. The relevant regime is the separate environmental pollution control framework. This principle applies by analogy to medicolegal cases where different regulatory frameworks govern distinct aspects of a claimant's exposure.

#### 2. Regulatory overlap and the limits of duty

The court held that the planning authority did not need to duplicate the functions of the environmental licensing regime. This is an important principle for medicolegal practice because clinical negligence exists within a broader regulatory context:

- The General Medical Council publishes guidance on professional conduct.
- The Care Quality Commission regulates healthcare providers.
- Professional bodies set clinical standards.
- Statutory regimes govern patient safety, infection control and data protection.

A breach of regulatory guidance does not automatically amount to clinical negligence. The medicolegal question remains whether the clinician's conduct fell below the standard of a reasonably competent practitioner, as judged by *Bolam* and *Bolitho*. Regulatory breach may inform the answer, but it does not determine it.

Conversely, compliance with regulation does not automatically defeat a negligence claim. A hospital may follow all regulatory guidance but still act negligently if a responsible body of clinicians would have taken additional precautions.

#### 3. Off-label use and clinical governance

One of the themes in the claimant's challenge was that the wall might enable intensified activity on the scrapyard, potentially increasing risk. In clinical practice, a similar issue arises when a healthcare organisation permits an activity that extends beyond its intended scope:

- **Off-label drug use.** Prescribing a medicine for an unlicensed indication carries increased medicolegal risk. The prescriber must justify the decision on an individual patient basis, with adequate information, consent and monitoring.
- **Procedural escalation.** A clinician who performs a procedure beyond their competencies or without adequate safeguarding may breach their duty of care even if the procedure is routinely performed by others.
- **Service reconfiguration.** Changes to clinical services that intensify workload, alter referral pathways or remove existing safeguards may create medicolegal risk if they increase the likelihood of error.

*Smith* illustrates that a planning authority is not required to investigate hypothetical future developments unsupported by evidence. In clinical practice, this means that a defendant trust is not automatically liable for negligent acts that a clinician might theoretically have committed in the future. The claimant must prove that a specific breach occurred and caused damage.

#### 4. The burden of proof and speculative claims

The claimant's challenge failed because the allegation that the wall would facilitate intensified use was unsupported by evidence. This parallels the evidential burden in medicolegal cases:

- **A possibility is not a probability.** A claimant must prove that, on the balance of probabilities, a different course of action would have produced a different outcome. Speculation that an investigation might have detected disease, or that a treatment might have succeeded, is insufficient.
- **Hypothetical developments are not justiciable.** The court will not determine what might have happened in an unproven future scenario. In clinical negligence, the court assesses breach at the time of the incident and causation by reference to a defined counterfactual.
- **Evidence is required.** The claimant must establish each link in the chain of causation. In *Sims v MacLennan* [2015], the claimant failed because she could not prove that untreated hypertension in 2002 would probably have caused the stroke in 2011. Similarly, in *Smith*, the claimant failed because they could not demonstrate that the wall would have increased scrapyard activity.

#### 5. Environmental contamination as a clinical negligence factor

Contamination exposure is not typically a clinical negligence issue, but it can intersect with clinical practice in several ways:

- **Failure to recognise and report.** A clinician who identifies occupational or environmental exposure risk must advise the patient and document the finding. A failure to do so may amount to clinical negligence if the patient suffers damage as a result.
- **Causation complexity.** Where a patient has multiple exposure sources (occupational, environmental, lifestyle), establishing the cause of injury requires expert toxicological and epidemiological evidence. Courts have recognised that where multiple factors cumulatively produce disease, the material-contribution principle may apply (*Cook v Bates* [2022] EWHC 829 (QB)).
- **Safeguarding and multi-agency working.** Where contamination affects a vulnerable patient, the clinician may have a duty to notify the relevant authority, coordinate with environmental health and ensure the patient understands the risk.
- **Informed consent.** If a patient requires treatment for a contamination-related condition (e.g., dialysis for heavy-metal poisoning, chelation therapy), the clinician must disclose the diagnosis, its source, the treatment options, and the patient's right to claim compensation.

### Key medicolegal takeaways from Smith

1. **Different regimes, different tests.** Environmental regulation, planning law and clinical negligence each have their own legal tests and standards. A breach in one regime does not automatically establish liability in another.

2. **The burden of proof remains on the claimant.** Whether challenging planning permission or alleging clinical negligence, the claimant must prove each element of the claim. Hypothetical or speculative arguments are insufficient.

3. **Compliance with regulation is relevant but not conclusive.** A defendant who follows professional guidance, regulatory requirements and best practice has a strong defence but is not automatically insulated from a negligence finding.

4. **Separation of function is important.** Planning authorities are not environmental regulators. Clinicians are not regulators. Each actor's duty is defined by their specific function and context.

5. **Evidence matters at every stage.** The claimant in *Smith* failed because the allegation was unsupported by evidence. In clinical negligence, the claimant must similarly produce expert evidence for breach and causation, and the court will test each claim against the standard of a reasonably competent practitioner.

### Relationship to other authorities

- **Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee** [1957] establishes the standard of care in clinical negligence. The medicolegal equivalent in environmental cases is the standard of a reasonably responsible person or organisation managing a site.
- **Bolitho v City and Hackney Health Authority** [1997] confirms that professional opinion must be logically defensible. The same principle applies to expert evidence in environmental contamination claims.
- **Sims v MacLennan** [2015] demonstrates the difficulty of proving causation where the claimant must establish a chain of hypothetical events. The evidential burden is similar regardless of the context.
- **Ternent v Ashford & St Peter's Hospital NHS Trust** [2010] illustrates the continuing importance of the burden of proof in medical negligence cases. The claimant bears the burden throughout; the defendant does not need to prove an alternative explanation.
