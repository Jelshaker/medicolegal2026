## Introduction

Musculoskeletal imaging frequently generates disputes when a later MRI, CT, surgery or specialist review identifies pathology that was not noted on an initial radiograph. While this sequence can naturally suggest reporting error, the legal analysis requires precise scrutiny. A subsequent diagnosis does not prove the original examination was abnormal, nor does it establish that a finding should have been visible on plain radiography. Courts must determine whether the initial study was technically adequate, what the images objectively demonstrated, whether the report fell within acceptable professional practice, whether further investigation was warranted, and whether any breach probably altered the clinical outcome.

## What Does “Unremarkable” Mean?

Terms like “normal,” “unremarkable” and “no acute bony abnormality” carry distinct meanings and must be interpreted contextually. “Normal” implies the absence of relevant pathology and should be used cautiously when anatomical coverage is limited or chronic changes are present. “Unremarkable” indicates no clinically significant finding was identified within the examination’s scope, acknowledging minor variations. “No acute bony abnormality” is narrower and appropriate when the clinical question concerns fracture or dislocation, but it does not exclude ligamentous, tendinous, meniscal, cartilaginous, marrow or early infectious processes. A normal report need not list every structure or document a complete search pattern, provided it addresses the clinical indication, accurately reflects the images, acknowledges material limitations, identifies significant findings, avoids overstating the examination’s capabilities, and recommends further investigation when clinically justified.

## The Legal Framework

Professional conduct in musculoskeletal reporting is assessed under the _Bolam_ principle, which permits reasonable differences in the recognition of subtle findings, interpretation of equivocal appearances, severity description, differential diagnosis and recommendations for further imaging. This professional deference is subject to _Bolitho_’s requirement for logical scrutiny, meaning supporting expert opinion must demonstrate a coherent basis rather than merely asserting that some practitioners would have issued the same report. As confirmed in _Brady v Southend University Hospital NHS Foundation Trust_, diagnostic interpretation follows the same _Bolam-Bolitho_ framework, requiring courts to distinguish between the factual question of what the images objectively showed and the professional question of whether the report was reasonably open to a competent practitioner. An eventual diagnosis different from the original opinion does not, without more, establish negligence.

## The Modality Question and Image Adequacy

Assessing a normal report requires first establishing what the original modality could reasonably demonstrate. Plain radiography effectively identifies displaced fractures, dislocations, alignment abnormalities, advanced degenerative change, focal bone lesions, certain joint effusions, calcification and indirect soft-tissue signs, but remains less sensitive for non-displaced or occult fractures, marrow oedema, ligament and tendon injury, meniscal or labral lesions, cartilage damage, early infection and subtle stress injuries. CT improves cortical and complex bony detail but still limits soft-tissue and marrow evaluation. MRI may reveal pathology entirely invisible on radiography, and experts must not treat later MRI findings as proof that the same lesion was apparent on the earlier study. The proper comparison asks whether the initial examination contained direct or indirect signs a competent reporter should have recognised. Before evaluating interpretation, experts must also determine whether the examination was technically adequate, considering anatomical coverage, projections, positioning, exposure, movement, rotation, artefact, inclusion of relevant joints, weight-bearing requirements and the availability of comparison studies. Inadequacy may stem from the referral, protocol selection, image acquisition or failure to request additional views, and an interpretation dispute should not obscure a fundamental acquisition problem.

## Clinical Information and Demonstrability

The clinical information supplied significantly influences how imaging appearances are interpreted. Relevant details include mechanism of injury, precise pain location, weight-bearing capacity, focal tenderness, mechanical symptoms, history of malignancy, systemic inflammatory signs, prior surgery, symptom duration and progression. While an inadequate referral does not excuse failure to identify a clear abnormality, it may affect examination selection, interpretive focus, the relevance of chronic changes and the need for further investigation. The medicolegal analysis must rely on the clinical context available to the original reporter, not a later reconstructed history. Regarding demonstrability, terms like “visible” or “subtle” lack fixed legal definitions. A finding may be apparent only after annotation, influenced by post-hoc knowledge, equivocal yet warranting mention, obscured by anatomy or technique, or genuinely absent. Experts should describe actual conspicuity using objective features such as size, location, contrast against adjacent structures, visibility across projections, cortical disruption, altered alignment and secondary soft-tissue signs. A finding does not become negligent simply because it can be pointed out retrospectively, but describing it as subtle does not automatically defend omission if its features should reasonably have prompted recognition or further investigation.

## Common Disputes and Language

Musculoskeletal claims frequently centre on occult fractures, subtle cortical or trabecular irregularities, alignment abnormalities, bone lesions and soft-tissue internal derangements. In each scenario, the analysis requires assessing whether the abnormality was present, whether indirect signs existed, whether the views were adequate, whether the clinical context suggested occult injury, and whether further imaging or immobilisation was indicated. Many soft-tissue and internal derangements are inherently invisible on plain radiography, meaning a normal report does not exclude significant pathology. Where the suspected condition falls outside the capability of the performed examination, the report must not imply exclusion. Language should reflect diagnostic confidence appropriately. Clear abnormalities warrant direct statements. Uncertainty may be conveyed using terms such as “possible,” “suspicious for,” “cannot exclude,” “equivocal” or “correlate with focal tenderness,” provided these expressions assist management rather than merely transferring uncertainty. Normal conclusions should not exceed the examination’s capabilities; for example, “no acute osseous abnormality” is often more precise than “normal knee” when menisci, ligaments and cartilage cannot be comprehensively assessed.

## Recommendations, Error Types and Causation

Radiologists are not required to recommend advanced imaging after every normal radiograph, but further investigation is appropriate when clinical indications suggest occult pathology, equivocal findings require clarification, the examination is technically limited, focal symptoms remain unexplained, results are discordant with the clinical picture, significant lesions require characterisation or established guidance supports additional study. The adequacy of such recommendations must be judged using the clinical information available at the time. Radiological discrepancies are often categorised as perceptual (abnormality present but missed) or cognitive (abnormality detected but mischaracterised or misattributed), but neither automatically establishes or excludes negligence. Both require assessment against reasonable professional practice. Crucially, breach and causation must be analysed separately. Even where an abnormality should have been identified, liability requires proof that competent reporting would probably have altered the treatment pathway or clinical outcome. The counterfactual must establish what the report should have stated, what action would have followed, when it would likely have occurred, what subsequent imaging or treatment would have revealed, and whether earlier intervention would probably have reduced injury, pain, deformity or functional loss. Specialist orthopaedic, spinal or rehabilitation evidence is often necessary to establish these downstream consequences.

## Evidence to Obtain

Disputed musculoskeletal reports typically require the complete original source images across all projections, the imaging request, the original report and any addenda, earlier and later studies, clinical and operative records, local protocols, applicable professional guidance, communication logs, treatment pathway documentation and specialist causation evidence. Screenshots and annotated images may aid explanation but should never replace review of the full original dataset. For radiologists, a defensible approach involves reviewing the clinical indication, verifying image adequacy before interpretation, maintaining a consistent search pattern, comparing prior studies where relevant, reporting significant incidental findings, stating material limitations, matching diagnostic confidence to appearances, recommending further imaging when reasonably indicated and avoiding conclusions beyond the modality’s capabilities. Reports need not list every normal structure but must clearly convey the clinically significant conclusion drawn from the examination. Expert witnesses should avoid both hindsight criticism and automatic deference, instead explaining image content, finding conspicuity, examination adequacy, the influence of clinical information, whether the original conclusion fell within responsible practice, whether supporting opinions are logically defensible and what difference a competent report would likely have made. Legal professionals should avoid treating the word “unremarkable” as breach in itself, focusing instead on examination scope, actual images, clinical question, finding significance, professional range, need for further imaging and causal consequences. A later abnormal MRI establishes diagnosis but does not, without more, prove negligent reporting of an earlier radiograph.

## Conclusion

Musculoskeletal reporting claims must not be reduced to a simplistic comparison between an initial normal report and a later abnormal examination. The correct approach requires determining whether the original examination was adequate, what the images objectively demonstrated, whether the report was reasonably open to a competent practitioner, whether modality limitations were respected, whether further investigation was required and whether any breach probably changed the outcome. _Bolam_ permits responsible professional variation, while _Bolitho_ demands logical defensibility. The standard remains reasonable practice under the circumstances, not retrospective perfection.
