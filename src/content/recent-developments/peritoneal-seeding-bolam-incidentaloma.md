## Introduction

Peritoneal metastases are frequently subtle, plaque-like, or infiltrative, often blending with normal peritoneal reflections, vessels, or postoperative change. The subsequent discovery of extensive disease at surgery or pathology does not prove that the same extent was visible on an earlier CT, as peritoneal malignancy can progress over time and operative inspection provides data that cross-sectional imaging cannot fully replicate. A medicolegal review must therefore systematically distinguish whether any abnormality was actually visible, whether it should have been recognised and characterised as malignant, whether the full disease extent could have been accurately staged, and whether earlier recognition would probably have altered management or outcome. This framework ensures that retrospective knowledge does not override contemporaneous diagnostic reality.

## Examination Specification & Clinical Information

The clinical context and technical parameters dictate the diagnostic utility of the study. A generic label such as “CT abdomen and pelvis” is insufficient without specifying the clinical indication, contrast administration, acquisition timing, anatomical coverage, slice thickness, image quality, and whether the examination was intended for diagnosis, staging, or surveillance. A study performed prior to a known malignancy should not automatically be characterised as a staging CT, though it still warrants review for any significant malignant features reasonably visible. The radiologist must interpret findings based on the contemporaneous clinical information available at the time, including symptoms, tumour markers, endoscopic findings, and the precise clinical question. While the absence of an explicit malignancy suspicion does not excuse a missed diagnosis, clinical details significantly influence protocol selection, the focus of review, and the interpretation of non-specific findings such as small-volume ascites or omental heterogeneity.

## CT Technique, Imaging Features & Distribution

Technical adequacy remains foundational. Intravenous contrast timing, typically in the portal venous phase, must be appropriate for the clinical question, and the absence of additional phases does not automatically render a study non-diagnostic for peritoneal disease. Multiplanar reconstructions, derived from thin-section volumetric datasets, are routinely available and should be utilised where possible, even if saved reformats are absent. The role of oral contrast or bowel preparation is situational and should not be deemed a breach without considering local protocols, urgency, and whether contrast would materially alter detection. Peritoneal metastases manifest through direct signs such as discrete nodules, plaque-like thickening, omental cake, and serosal implants, as well as indirect signs including unexplained ascites, bowel tethering, mesenteric distortion, and solid-organ surface scalloping. These findings are rarely specific in isolation; interpretation depends on the overall pattern. A systematic review should encompass subphrenic spaces, diaphragmatic surfaces, omenta, mesenteric root, bowel serosa, and the pelvis. Small-bowel serosal and mesenteric disease is clinically significant for surgical planning but notoriously difficult to stage accurately on CT, and the failure to depict every operative implant does not necessarily constitute negligence.

## Detection, Characterisation, Staging & Conspicuity

Radiological assessment must distinguish between detection, characterisation, and staging. Detection asks whether an abnormality should have been identified; characterisation asks whether it could reasonably be regarded as suspicious for malignancy rather than benign change; and staging asks whether the examination could reliably define disease burden. A CT may identify possible peritoneal disease while underestimating its extent, and small-volume non-specific free fluid does not equate to metastatic diagnosis. The expert must avoid leaping from “visible” to “malignant” without analysing the intermediate reasoning. Conspicuity depends on multiple factors beyond mere size, including distribution, attenuation, enhancement, contrast with surrounding fat, visibility across projections, and association with a primary tumour or corroborating abnormalities. No universal size threshold determines negligence, as a tiny deposit surrounded by ascites may be more apparent than a larger plaque against dense bowel. The expert should describe the actual appearance in context rather than relying on unsupported numerical cut-offs.

## Hindsight Bias & Legal Framework

Interpretation is inevitably clouded by hindsight when later surgery, pathology, or advanced imaging reveals the diagnosis. The expert must review the original examination before consulting later studies, isolate the contemporaneous clinical information, and assess conspicuity without assuming operative findings were already known. It is crucial to account for disease progression during the interval and clearly label findings as specific, suspicious, equivocal, or non-specific. Retrospective visibility does not equate to negligence, nor does describing a finding as “subtle” automatically defend an omission if a coherent pattern of primary tumour, ascites, and peritoneal thickening warranted concern. The legal standard is governed by _Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee_ as refined by _Bolitho v City and Hackney Health Authority_. The court must distinguish the factual question of what the images showed from the professional question of whether the interpretation and recommendations fell within a responsible, logically defensible range of practice. Expert evidence must explain why a competent body could reasonably have classified appearances as benign or non-specific, rather than relying on blanket assertions that “others would have missed it.”

## Breach Analysis, Reporting & Limitations

The classification of a finding as incidental depends on the clinical context. If the examination was requested to investigate suspected malignancy or unexplained symptoms, peritoneal disease forms part of the principal diagnostic task. Regardless of classification, the radiologist should review the acquired examination as a whole and identify significant abnormalities reasonably apparent within it, without requiring exhaustive description of every benign variation. A structured breach analysis should determine whether the examination was clinically appropriate and technically adequate, what the images objectively demonstrated, what a competent report should have stated, and whether further investigation was warranted. Potential conclusions range from definitively negative to suspicious, indeterminate, or limited. Where material technical limitations exist, the report should explicitly state what could not be reliably assessed, why, and what further investigation is recommended, avoiding generic disclaimers. The presence of ascites alone is non-specific; its significance depends on clinical context, volume, distribution, associated peritoneal or omental changes, and interval evolution. Similarly, if a primary tumour was later diagnosed, the earlier CT must be reviewed for bowel wall thickening, shouldering, regional lymphadenopathy, and synchronous disease, as a missed primary may provide an independent pathway to breach and causation.

## Causation, Emergency Presentation & Evidence

Even where a reporting error is established, liability requires proof that earlier recognition would probably have altered the outcome. The counterfactual timeline must address what a competent report should have stated, when it would have been communicated, what investigation or referral would follow, when malignancy would likely have been confirmed, the probable stage and resectability at that time, and whether earlier treatment would likely have improved prognosis. The mere presence of peritoneal metastases does not guarantee that cytoreductive surgery with hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy would have been offered or successful, as patient selection depends on tumour biology, disease distribution, mesenteric involvement, performance status, and multidisciplinary assessment. If the later diagnosis followed an emergency presentation such as bowel obstruction, the expert must distinguish the underlying malignancy from the acute complication, assess whether the complication was already present on the earlier CT, and determine whether planned treatment would likely have avoided emergency surgery and its associated morbidity. Essential evidence includes the complete original DICOM dataset, acquisition parameters, clinical request, contemporaneous and subsequent reports, operative and histopathology records, local protocols, and specialist oncological and surgical evidence on treatment pathways and prognosis.

## Practical Guidance

Radiologists should adopt a systematic approach that prioritises clinical indication, verifies technical adequacy, examines the peritoneum and omentum methodically, correlates direct and indirect signs, distinguishes suspicion from certainty, states material limitations, and recommends further investigation when appropriate. Expert witnesses must avoid both retrospective overcalling and uncritical defence, clearly separating detection, characterisation, and staging while explaining which causation questions fall outside radiological expertise. Legal professionals should frame instructions around what the original examination objectively showed, the contemporaneous conspicuity of findings, technical appropriateness, the reasonableness of the differential diagnosis, the likely diagnostic and treatment timeline under competent care, and the probable impact on patient outcome. This structured approach separates the image-reading dispute from the clinical counterfactual.

## Conclusion

Peritoneal metastases are frequently subtle and challenging to detect, characterise, or stage accurately on CT. The medicolegal test does not ask whether disease can be identified after surgery or pathology has revealed the diagnosis, but whether the original examination, interpreted within its contemporaneous clinical and technical context, was reported in accordance with responsible professional practice. Technical limitations require explicit acknowledgment but do not automatically excuse a negative report, while retrospective visibility never equates to negligence. Each case remains highly fact-specific, requiring careful separation of detection, characterisation, staging, breach, and causation, with radiological opinion precisely scoped to imaging adequacy, conspicuousness, and probable reporting timelines, while leaving surgical, oncological, and long-term prognostic questions to appropriately qualified specialists.
