## The clinical scenario

A 62-year-old male presented with a two-month history of altered bowel habit and
early satiety. A CT abdomen (single IV bolus, no oral contrast, no portal
venous reconstructions) was reported by a trust radiologist as "non-contributory.
No liver metastases or lymphadenopathy." The report did mention "traces of free
fluid in the pelvis — likely benign."

The patient was discharged. Three months later, he presented with a small bowel
obstruction. Emergency laparotomy revealed diffuse peritoneal carcinomatosis
secondary to a caecal primary. He underwent a right hemicolectomy with HIPEC
procedure.

The family instructed a surgical expert who concluded the delay was "materially
detrimental" and then instructed a radiologist to review the initial CT. The
retrospective radiological opinion concluded that "subtle peritoneal nodularity
and omental stranding were present and consistent with peritoneal seeding."

The claimant's solicitors served a CPR Part 35 report asserting that the original
radiologist breached the Bolam standard and caused a delay in staging that
materially altered the treatment pathway.

## The Bolam test applied to peritoneal imaging

The Bolam test remains the bedrock of radiological negligence defence in the UK:

> "A man need not be censured if he acts in accordance with a practice
> accepted as proper by a responsible body of men skilled in that particular art."
>
> — *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582

This means the expert must identify a body of competent radiologists who, on the
same images with the same clinical context, would have reached the same
conclusion. The question is not whether a peritoneal implant was visible
*in hindsight* — perhaps on a subsequent FDG-PET scan or at laparotomy — but
whether the finding was demonstrable on the study as performed and reviewed on
the day.

### The challenge of peritoneal CT imaging

Peritoneal metastases are notoriously subtle on CT:

- **Diameter threshold:** Most studies suggest peritoneal implants must exceed
  5 mm to be confidently identified on CT with any degree of sensitivity.
- **Contrast timing:** Portal venous phase imaging with multi-planar
  reconstructions (coronal and sagittal reformats) significantly increases
  sensitivity for peritoneal disease. A single-phase axial study is inherently
  limited.
- **Bowel preparation:** Without adequate oral contrast or water distension,
  distinguishing peritoneal implants from peristaltic folds or adherent bowel
  is extremely difficult.
- **Omental disease:** Omental caking and "omental cake" are more readily
  identifiable than individual peritoneal implants — but early disease may
  manifest only as subtle nodularity along the diaphragmatic surface or
  paracolic gutters.

### Incidentaloma doctrine and the RCR Standards

The allegation in this case was that the radiologist breached his duty by
failing to report "subtle peritoneal seeding" visible in retrospect — a form of
"incidentaloma" argument: the idea that if a lesion is seen by the expert at a
later date, the original reporter was negligent for missing it.

This argument confronts two important frameworks:

**RCR Standards 2018** require that "radiologists should comment on significant
incidental findings in all body regions visible within the scanned field."
However, this duty is tempered by:

- The **indication** for the study (in this case, a general survey without
  specific suspicion of peritoneal disease),
- The **technical adequacy** of the images for detecting peritoneal implants,
- The **practical reality** that a single-phase, non-reconstructed study has
  well-documented limitations in this context.

**RCR Standards (October 2025 update)** add that:

> "When the referral information is limited, the radiologist should record the
> limitations of the study and recommend appropriate further imaging where the
> clinical suspicion is high and the current study is non-diagnostic."

This is a crucial addition. In our case, the original report stated "traces of
free fluid in the pelvis — likely benign" but did *not* recommend further imaging
despite the clinical context of altered bowel habit and early satiety — symptoms
that should raise suspicion of peritoneal disease in a 62-year-old.

## Was there a breach?

This is where the expert's analysis must be structured:

### Step 1: Was the lesion demonstrable?

Re-derivation of the source DICOM revealed:

- The study was indeed a single-phase acquisition without reconstructions.
- There was mild stranding of the greater omentum, but no discrete omental cake.
- No peritoneal deposits exceeding 3–4 mm were identified.
- Ascitic fluid was present in the pelvis but no loculations.

The retrospective expert's assertion that "subtle peritoneal nodularity" was
present was based on the hindsight knowledge that the patient subsequently had
peritoneal carcinomatosis. But at 3–4 mm, without reconstructions, and without
oral contrast, this finding sits at the threshold of CT detectability.

### Step 2: Would a competent body of radiologists agree?

A responsible body of abdominal radiologists would be expected to flag:

- Ascitic fluid in a patient with concerning symptoms,
- Omental stranding as a potential non-specific finding,
- The limitation of the study in excluding peritoneal disease.

In this case, the report *did* mention the ascitic fluid. The question is whether
it went far enough. I would argue it was reasonable to describe it as "likely
benign" in the absence of other stigmata of peritoneal disease — but the absence
of a recommendation for further imaging when the clinical picture was suspicious
is where the argument for breach becomes stronger.

### Step 3: Causation

The causation analysis is separate. Even if a breach is found, was the delay
materially detrimental? At the time of the initial CT, the disease was likely
already at stage IV. The question is whether an earlier diagnosis would have
allowed a different treatment — perhaps upfront cytoreduction and HIPEC rather
than emergency laparotomy for obstruction.

## Key learning points

1. **Hindsight bias is the enemy of fair medicolegal analysis.** The fact that
   peritoneal disease was found at surgery does not prove the CT was
   non-diagnostic on the day.

2. **Study technique matters enormously.** A single-phase, non-reconstructed CT
   has well-defined limitations for peritoneal staging. The expert must always
   evaluate the protocol before judging the report.

3. **The Bolam defence is strongest when the study was genuinely limited.** A
   radiologist cannot be expected to see what no responsible body of peers would
   have seen on the same images.

4. **Incidental finding duties are bounded by clinical context.** The RCR Standards
   do not require a radiologist to perform a full oncology staging investigation
   on a study that was never designed to be one — but they *do* require
   appropriate flagging and recommendation when clinical suspicion warrants it.

5. **The 2025 RCR update strengthens the argument for "non-diagnostic" reporting.**
   Where a study is technically inadequate for the suspected condition, the expert
   witness should be prepared to defend the use of a qualified conclusion
   ("peritoneal disease cannot be excluded on this study") rather than a
   reassuring negative.
