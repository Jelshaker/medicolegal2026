**Jarman v Brighton and Sussex University Hospitals NHS Trust**

[2021] EWHC 323 (QB)

## Issue

appropriateness of triage delay for emergency diagnostic imaging — urgent versus immediate MRI.

## Facts

a patient presenting to A&E with severe back pain was given an "urgent" MRI within two weeks rather than an immediate emergency scan. The claimant alleged developing cauda equina syndrome required same-day imaging.

## Principle

claim dismissed under *Bolam* (Scottish root: *Hunter v Hanley 1955 SC 200*). Deferring immediate MRI was acceptable because the patient lacked overt, severe red-flag signs at initial examination, and a responsible body of clinicians would have triaged the same way. Distinguish *Darnley* ([case-law index 13](/radiology-legal-cases#triage-delay)), where the breach was misinformation deterring attendance, and *Grimes* ([case-law index 12](/radiology-legal-cases#adequate-views)), where views obtained were inadequate as a matter of fact. MDU data (*article-1789575458816.pdf* extract: cauda equina among the commonest delayed-diagnosis allegations) explains why back-pain triage carries disproportionate claims exposure even when defensible.

## Why it matters — radiology

"urgent" and "emergency" MRI are different promises, and triage notes are the evidence. Jarman protects a reasoned deferral grounded in contemporaneous red-flag assessment — but only where that assessment is recorded.

## For consultant radiologists

vetting is a clinical act: document which red flags were asked about, what was absent, and what safety-netting was given. Agree vetting criteria with ED and primary care so "urgent within two weeks" has a defensible definition.

## For legal professionals

Jarman turns on the examination record, not the images. Test what red flags were documented at triage and whether any responsible body would have deferred — Darnley-style misinformation is a different and easier route to breach.
