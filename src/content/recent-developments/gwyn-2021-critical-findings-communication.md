## Introduction

A radiology report alone holds limited clinical value if a significant finding fails to reach the clinician responsible for acting on it. While routine results can be conveyed through standard electronic pathways, urgent, unexpected, or clinically critical findings require additional, proportionate communication. The response must align with the clinical risk, time sensitivity, and patient setting, underpinned by a system explicitly designed to catch overlooked results.

## A System-Wide Responsibility

Failures in imaging-result communication rarely stem from a single point of breakdown. The pathway typically spans the referrer, imaging acquisition team, authorised reporter, electronic alert systems, receiving clinical teams, administrative services, and the clinician informing the patient. A robust system must explicitly define responsibility at each handoff, particularly when staff are unavailable, patients transition between teams, or acknowledgements are delayed or missing.

## The Reporter’s Responsibility

The radiologist or authorised reporter must assess whether routine electronic delivery suffices or if direct escalation is warranted. This decision hinges on the finding’s severity, potential for rapid harm, expectedness, patient location, referrer availability, pathway reliability, and consequences of delay. When escalation is required, the agreed local route must be used, with clear documentation of the finding, timing, method, recipient, and any advised action. The reporter’s duty is to communicate time-critical results with appropriate clarity and urgency, not to manage clinical treatment.

## The Clinical Team’s Responsibility

The receiving team must maintain reliable processes for reviewing reports, acknowledging alerts, acting on recommendations, and arranging follow-up care. An automated alert does not constitute pathway closure; it must reach a competent recipient who clinically reviews and responds. While clinicians may legitimately deviate from radiological advice, such decisions must be evidence-based and documented. Continuity of care and explicit handovers between teams are essential to prevent dropped results.

## The Provider’s Responsibility

Healthcare organisations must establish and enforce documented protocols for managing significant imaging findings. These should cover trigger criteria, communication methods, urgency tiers, out-of-hours coverage, escalation pathways, acknowledgement tracking, addendum handling, patient communication, and cross-boundary care. While electronic systems enhance safety, they cannot replace defined human accountability. Automated notifications must not be presumed to confirm reading, comprehension, or clinical action.

## Urgent and Unexpected Findings

Not all abnormalities require direct contact, but the communication method must reflect the consequences of delay. Immediately time-critical findings, such as unstable intracranial haemorrhage, tension pneumothorax, or major vascular occlusion, demand prompt direct communication to an appropriate clinician. Significant but non-immediate findings, like suspected new malignancies, require proactive attention rather than passive inbox review, with local systems ensuring timely escalation. Similarly, clinically material addenda released after initial reporting should trigger active notification if they alter patient management.

## Closed-Loop Communication

Effective result communication follows a closed loop: issuance, directed notification, acknowledgement, clinical review, and documented action. Robust systems must also flag incomplete loops where acknowledgment or action is missing. Organisational protocols will vary, but the underlying principle remains consistent: the pathway must not rely on the assumption that another party has already received and addressed the result.

## Medicolegal Analysis

Claims stemming from communication failures require precise identification of where the pathway broke down. Key considerations include correct finding identification, appropriate escalation, documentation, recipient competence, acknowledgement status, reasonableness of clinical response, system adequacy, and causation. Crucially, the communication breakdown and its clinical consequences must be evaluated separately, with claimants typically required to demonstrate that earlier receipt and action would likely have altered the outcome.

## Evidence to Obtain

Reconstructing the communication pathway often outweighs imaging re-review in medicolegal cases. Relevant documentation includes reports, verification timestamps, alert logs, acknowledgment records, phone and secure message histories, EHR access logs, referral and clinic notes, local policies, staff rotas, incident reports, and evidence of subsequent management. Importantly, an absent acknowledgment does not prove non-receipt, nor does a sent alert guarantee clinician awareness or action.

## Practical Principles

Radiologists should adhere to local escalation protocols, match communication urgency to clinical risk, target identifiable recipients, thoroughly document all critical exchanges, escalate when standard contacts are unreachable, and treat material addenda as new communication events when management may change. Clinical teams must maintain vigilant report review processes, ensure reliable coverage for absent staff, document responses to significant findings, clarify responsibility during care transitions, and appropriately inform patients. Providers must clearly delineate pathway responsibilities, implement proportionate acknowledgement and fail-safe mechanisms, audit action rates for critical findings, and treat failures as systemic issues rather than assuming report issuance fulfills clinical duty.

## Conclusion

Safe imaging communication extends far beyond electronic report submission. It requires a coordinated pathway spanning identification, notification, receipt, acknowledgement, review, and action, with shared accountability among reporters, clinical teams, and healthcare organisations. When adverse outcomes occur, analysis must pinpoint where the loop broke and whether timely communication would likely have changed the clinical trajectory.
