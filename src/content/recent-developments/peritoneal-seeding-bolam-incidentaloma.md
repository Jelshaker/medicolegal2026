## Introduction

Peritoneal metastases can be difficult to identify and stage on CT.

Disease may be small, plaque-like or infiltrative rather than forming a discrete mass. Deposits may lie against bowel, mesentery, diaphragm or solid organs and may be difficult to distinguish from vessels, collapsed bowel, postoperative change, inflammation or normal peritoneal reflections.

The eventual discovery of extensive disease at surgery does not establish that the same extent was visible on an earlier CT. Peritoneal malignancy may progress during the interval, and operative inspection and palpation provide information that cross-sectional imaging cannot reproduce completely.

A medicolegal review should therefore distinguish:

1. whether any abnormality was visible;
2. whether it should have been recognised;
3. whether it could reasonably have been characterised as malignant;
4. whether the full extent of disease could have been staged accurately; and
5. whether earlier recognition would probably have changed management or outcome.

## Illustrative Scenario

The following anonymised composite scenario illustrates the analytical method. It is not a report of a published judgment or an identifiable instruction.

An adult patient underwent contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen and pelvis following altered bowel habit, abdominal discomfort and weight loss.

The examination was reported as showing no definite malignancy. A small volume of pelvic free fluid and mild omental heterogeneity were described as non-specific.

Several months later, the patient presented with bowel obstruction. Further investigation demonstrated a caecal malignancy with peritoneal metastatic disease.

Retrospective review of the earlier CT identified subtle omental nodularity and possible focal peritoneal thickening. It was alleged that the original reporter should have diagnosed or suspected peritoneal metastases and recommended further investigation.

The questions for the radiology expert were:

- whether the primary tumour was demonstrated;
- whether peritoneal disease was visible;
- how conspicuous the abnormalities were;
- whether the original conclusion was reasonably open to a competent radiologist;
- whether further investigation was required; and
- whether earlier reporting would probably have changed treatment or outcome.

## Clarify the Examination

The first task is to establish what examination was actually performed.

The label “CT abdomen and pelvis” does not identify:

- the clinical indication;
- whether malignancy was known or suspected;
- contrast administration;
- acquisition timing;
- anatomical coverage;
- bowel distension;
- slice thickness;
- available reconstructions;
- image quality;
- relevant prior imaging; or
- whether the examination was intended for diagnosis, staging, surveillance or assessment of an acute complication.

A study performed before the diagnosis of malignancy should not automatically be described as a staging CT.

Conversely, a general diagnostic examination should still be reviewed for significant malignant features reasonably visible on the images.

## Clinical Information

The expert should review the original request and the information reasonably available to the reporter.

Relevant details may include:

- altered bowel habit;
- weight loss;
- anaemia;
- abdominal distension;
- early satiety;
- palpable mass;
- previous malignancy;
- previous abdominal surgery;
- inflammatory markers;
- tumour markers where available;
- endoscopic findings;
- suspected bowel obstruction; and
- the precise clinical question.

The absence of an explicit suspicion of malignancy does not excuse failure to identify a clear tumour.

Clinical information may nevertheless affect:

- protocol selection;
- the focus of review;
- the significance attached to small-volume ascites;
- interpretation of omental or mesenteric change;
- the differential diagnosis; and
- whether additional investigation should be recommended.

The expert should use the information recorded at the relevant time rather than a history reconstructed after the diagnosis.

## CT Technique

The technical review should identify:

- whether intravenous contrast was administered;
- the timing and adequacy of enhancement;
- slice thickness;
- axial and multiplanar reconstructions;
- anatomical coverage;
- motion or other artefact;
- bowel distension;
- postoperative change;
- patient habitus;
- presence of ascites; and
- relevant prior studies.

### Contrast phase

Portal venous phase CT is commonly used for abdominal and oncological assessment. Its adequacy depends on the primary tumour, clinical question and suspected complication.

Additional phases may be required for particular indications, but their absence does not automatically make a study non-diagnostic for peritoneal disease.

### Multiplanar reconstructions

Coronal and sagittal reformats may improve appreciation of disease distribution and anatomical relationships.

They are reconstructed from the acquired volumetric dataset. The absence of saved reformats does not necessarily mean that multiplanar review was impossible, provided suitable thin-section source data were available.

The expert should establish what images and tools were available to the original reporter rather than assuming that review was confined to thicker axial images.

### Oral contrast and bowel preparation

Oral contrast or neutral bowel distension may assist in selected circumstances. It is not universally required for detection of peritoneal metastases.

Its absence should not be described as a breach without considering:

- the indication;
- local protocol;
- contemporary practice;
- the primary tumour;
- urgency;
- the suspected disease distribution; and
- whether oral contrast would probably have altered interpretation.

An “unprepared” examination may be less suitable for detailed assessment of bowel or small serosal deposits, but that limitation must be explained rather than assumed.

## Imaging Features of Peritoneal Disease

Peritoneal metastases may produce direct or indirect findings.

### Direct findings

These include:

- discrete peritoneal nodules;
- plaque-like thickening;
- peritoneal enhancement;
- omental nodularity;
- omental cake;
- implants along organ surfaces;
- deposits in dependent spaces; and
- tumour involving the mesentery or serosa.

### Indirect findings

These may include:

- otherwise unexplained ascites;
- tethering or clustering of bowel loops;
- focal transition points;
- mesenteric distortion;
- scalloping of solid-organ surfaces;
- hydronephrosis or ureteric obstruction;
- fixed bowel segments;
- multifocal obstruction; and
- disproportionate omental or mesenteric stranding.

These findings are not individually specific.

Ascites alone does not establish peritoneal malignancy. Omental stranding may reflect inflammation, infarction, oedema, postoperative change or another benign process. Small nodules may represent lymph nodes, vessels, splenosis or other mimics.

The interpretation depends on the pattern as a whole.

## Disease Distribution

Peritoneal deposits may be easier to detect in some locations than others.

A systematic review may include:

- subphrenic spaces;
- diaphragmatic surfaces;
- liver and splenic capsules;
- greater and lesser omentum;
- paracolic gutters;
- mesenteric root;
- small-bowel mesentery;
- bowel serosa;
- pelvis and pouch of Douglas;
- pelvic sidewalls;
- lesser sac; and
- surfaces around the primary tumour.

Small-bowel serosal and mesenteric disease is particularly important when determining suitability for cytoreductive surgery, but it may be difficult to stage accurately on CT.

Failure to depict every operative implant does not necessarily mean that the CT was negligently reported.

## Detection, Characterisation and Staging

These are different tasks.

### Detection

Was there an abnormality that a competent radiologist should have identified?

### Characterisation

Could the abnormality reasonably have been recognised as suspicious for peritoneal metastasis rather than benign or non-specific change?

### Staging

Could the examination reliably establish the distribution and burden of disease?

A CT may reasonably identify possible peritoneal disease while underestimating its extent.

Equally, a small volume of non-specific free fluid may be detectable without being sufficient to support a diagnosis of metastatic disease.

The expert should avoid moving directly from “visible” to “malignant” without analysing the intermediate reasoning.

## Conspicuity

The expert should describe how apparent the disputed finding was on the original examination.

Relevant factors include:

- size;
- number;
- distribution;
- attenuation;
- enhancement;
- contrast with surrounding fat or fluid;
- visibility on more than one image or plane;
- association with a primary tumour;
- associated ascites;
- distortion of normal anatomy;
- presence of corroborating abnormalities;
- image quality; and
- change from prior imaging.

A precise universal size threshold should not be used to determine negligence.

Small implants are generally more difficult to detect, but lesion size is only one factor. A tiny deposit surrounded by ascites or omental fat may be more conspicuous than a larger plaque against bowel or solid organ.

The opinion should explain the appearance in the particular examination rather than relying on an unsupported numerical cut-off.

## Hindsight Bias

Knowledge of later surgery, pathology or advanced imaging changes the way an earlier study is viewed.

The expert should therefore:

- review the original examination before reviewing later imaging where practicable;
- identify what clinical information was available at the time;
- avoid using operative findings as though they were already known;
- distinguish direct signs from findings visible only after annotation;
- account for disease progression during the interval; and
- explain whether the original features were specific, suspicious, equivocal or non-specific.

A finding may be identifiable retrospectively without having been sufficiently conspicuous or specific to make omission negligent.

The reverse is also true. Calling a finding “subtle” does not answer the legal question if a coherent pattern of primary tumour, ascites, omental nodularity and peritoneal thickening should reasonably have prompted concern.

## The Legal Test

The professional standard is assessed under *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582, subject to *Bolitho v City and Hackney Health Authority* [1998] AC 232.

The issue is whether the original report fell within a responsible and logically defensible range of professional practice.

The court must distinguish:

1. the factual question of what the images demonstrated; and
2. the professional question of whether the interpretation and recommendations were reasonable.

It is not necessary for the defendant to identify radiologists who would reproduce the exact report word for word.

The expert evidence should instead explain whether a responsible body of competent radiologists could reasonably have:

- regarded the appearances as benign or non-specific;
- omitted peritoneal malignancy from the differential;
- issued a negative conclusion;
- recommended no further investigation; or
- reached the same conclusion despite uncertainty.

Professional opinion must be reasoned. Assertion that “others would also have missed it” is not sufficient without analysis of the images and clinical context.

## Incidental Finding or Missed Target Pathology?

The expression “incidental finding” should be used carefully.

If the examination was requested to investigate possible malignancy, unexplained symptoms or a known gastrointestinal tumour, peritoneal metastatic disease may form part of the principal diagnostic task rather than being incidental.

The alleged finding is more properly described as incidental where:

- it is unrelated to the clinical indication;
- the examination was performed for another organ or condition; and
- there was no known or suspected primary malignancy relevant to the peritoneum.

The classification matters because it affects the clinical context, but it does not determine breach.

A clinically important abnormality may require reporting even if incidental. Conversely, not every retrospectively visible incidental feature should have been detected or characterised.

## Findings Outside the Clinical Question

A radiologist should review the acquired examination as a whole and identify significant abnormalities reasonably apparent within it.

That does not require:

- exhaustive description of every benign variation;
- equal diagnostic sensitivity for every organ;
- characterisation beyond the study's technical capability; or
- recommendations based solely on remote possibilities.

The appropriate question is whether the finding was sufficiently apparent and important that omission fell outside responsible practice.

## A Structured Breach Analysis

### 1. Was the examination appropriate?

- What was the clinical question?
- Was the selected CT protocol reasonable?
- Was malignancy known or suspected?
- Was the study technically adequate?

### 2. What did the images show?

- Was there a primary tumour?
- Was ascites present?
- Was there omental or peritoneal nodularity?
- Were there indirect signs of serosal or mesenteric disease?
- How conspicuous and specific were the findings?

### 3. What should the report have said?

Possible conclusions might include:

- no evidence of peritoneal metastatic disease;
- indeterminate small-volume ascites;
- non-specific omental change;
- appearances suspicious for peritoneal metastases;
- further investigation advised; or
- limited assessment of the peritoneum on this examination.

The appropriate conclusion depends on the actual study.

### 4. Was further investigation required?

Potential next steps may include:

- repeat contrast-enhanced CT;
- dedicated oncological staging CT;
- MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging;
- endoscopy;
- image-guided biopsy;
- multidisciplinary review;
- surgical assessment; or
- interval imaging.

The expert should explain why a competent radiologist would or would not have recommended the proposed next step.

## Limitations and Qualified Reporting

A technically limited study should not automatically produce a reassuring negative conclusion.

Where the limitation is material, the report may need to state:

- what could not be assessed reliably;
- why assessment was limited;
- whether important disease remains possible; and
- what further investigation is recommended.

However, reports should not contain generic disclaimers for every theoretical limitation.

A qualified conclusion should be used where the limitation genuinely affects the clinical question and patient management.

## The Significance of Ascites

Small-volume pelvic fluid is not specific for peritoneal malignancy.

Its significance depends on:

- age and sex;
- clinical symptoms;
- known malignancy;
- recent surgery;
- cardiac, hepatic or inflammatory disease;
- volume and distribution;
- associated peritoneal enhancement;
- omental or mesenteric abnormalities;
- bowel findings; and
- interval change.

The expert should not assume that fluid later associated with carcinomatosis was necessarily malignant at the earlier date.

Equally, unexplained ascites in an older patient with concerning symptoms and additional suspicious findings may warrant a more cautious conclusion or further investigation.

## Primary Tumour Detection

Where a colorectal primary was later diagnosed, the expert should examine the earlier CT for:

- focal or annular mural thickening;
- shouldering;
- luminal narrowing;
- upstream dilatation;
- adjacent stranding;
- regional lymphadenopathy;
- local invasion;
- synchronous lesions;
- liver metastases; and
- peritoneal disease.

The question is not limited to the peritoneum.

If a primary tumour was reasonably visible and should have prompted colonoscopy or specialist referral, that may provide a separate route to breach and causation.

## Causation

Even where the report was negligent, liability requires proof that earlier diagnosis would probably have made a material difference.

The counterfactual should identify:

1. what the competent report should have said;
2. when the result would have been communicated;
3. what investigation or referral would probably have followed;
4. when malignancy would probably have been confirmed;
5. the likely stage and resectability at that time;
6. what treatment would probably have been offered; and
7. whether earlier treatment would probably have improved the outcome.

The presence of peritoneal metastases does not by itself establish that earlier treatment would have been curative or that cytoreductive surgery with hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy would have been offered.

Patient selection depends on factors including:

- tumour origin and biology;
- disease distribution;
- small-bowel and mesenteric involvement;
- extra-peritoneal disease;
- anticipated completeness of cytoreduction;
- performance status;
- comorbidity; and
- multidisciplinary assessment.

A radiologist may assist with imaging stage and disease distribution. Surgical suitability, treatment choice and prognosis ordinarily require evidence from relevant oncological and surgical specialists.

## Emergency Presentation

If the later diagnosis followed bowel obstruction or perforation, the expert should distinguish:

- the underlying malignancy;
- the development of the acute complication;
- whether the complication was already present on the earlier CT;
- whether earlier diagnosis would probably have avoided the emergency;
- whether the same operation would still have been required; and
- whether emergency presentation altered treatment, morbidity or prognosis.

A later emergency does not prove that the earlier CT was negligently reported.

It may nevertheless be relevant to causation if competent reporting would probably have led to planned treatment before obstruction or perforation developed.

## Evidence to Obtain

A peritoneal malignancy claim will commonly require:

- the complete original DICOM examination;
- all source images and reconstructions;
- the imaging request and clinical details;
- the original report and any addenda;
- contrast and acquisition information;
- relevant prior imaging;
- later CT, MRI or PET/CT;
- endoscopic findings;
- operative and laparoscopic findings;
- histopathology;
- multidisciplinary-team records;
- oncology and surgical treatment plans;
- local imaging protocols;
- professional guidance applicable at the time; and
- expert evidence on treatment, resectability and prognosis.

Screenshots and selected annotated images may assist explanation but should not replace review of the full original examination.

## Research on Incidental Findings

A retrospective review of 112 medicolegal cases from five abdominal-radiology expert witnesses found that 41 allegations concerned failure to report incidental pathology.

The most common incidental allegation was failure to identify tumour.

Importantly, only 12 of the 41 incidental-finding interpretations were judged substandard by the reviewing experts.

This was a selected expert-witness series rather than a national claims database. It does not mean that 37% of all abdominal-radiology claims concern incidental findings.

The study supports two conclusions:

- incidental findings are a frequent subject of allegations; and
- most allegations in the reviewed series did not amount to substandard interpretation.

Presence on retrospective review and negligence are not equivalent.

## Practical Point for Radiologists

A defensible approach to possible peritoneal malignancy includes:

- reviewing the clinical indication;
- checking whether technique is appropriate;
- examining the peritoneum and omentum systematically;
- comparing relevant prior studies;
- assessing direct and indirect signs together;
- distinguishing suspicion from definite disease;
- stating material technical limitations;
- recommending further investigation where reasonably indicated;
- avoiding reassurance beyond the capability of the examination; and
- communicating significant unexpected findings appropriately.

The report should answer the clinical question while remaining alert to important abnormalities elsewhere in the examination.

## Practical Point for Expert Witnesses

The expert should avoid both retrospective overcalling and uncritical defence of the original report.

The opinion should explain:

- what the original CT demonstrated;
- what was not shown;
- the conspicuity and specificity of the alleged disease;
- the relevance of the clinical information;
- the effect of the protocol and image quality;
- whether a competent body could reasonably have reached the original conclusion;
- whether that opinion is logically defensible;
- what further investigation should have been advised; and
- which causation questions fall outside radiological expertise.

The expert should distinguish detection, characterisation and staging throughout.

## Practical Point for Legal Professionals

The instruction should not ask only:

> Can the disease now be seen on the earlier scan?

It should ask:

1. What did the earlier examination objectively demonstrate?
2. How conspicuous were the relevant findings without hindsight?
3. Was the study appropriate for the clinical question?
4. Was the primary tumour visible?
5. Was peritoneal malignancy a reasonable differential?
6. Should further investigation have been recommended?
7. When would diagnosis probably have occurred with competent care?
8. What treatment would then probably have been available?
9. Would that treatment probably have improved the outcome?

Those questions separate the image-reading dispute from the clinical and oncological counterfactual.

## Conclusion

Peritoneal metastases may be present yet difficult to detect, characterise or stage accurately on CT.

The legal test is not whether disease can be identified after surgery, pathology or later imaging has revealed the diagnosis.

The expert must determine:

- what examination was performed;
- what clinical information was available;
- what the original images showed;
- how apparent and specific the abnormalities were;
- whether the report and recommendations fell within responsible practice; and
- what earlier recognition would probably have changed.

Technical limitations matter, but they do not automatically excuse a negative report. A competent reporter should recognise material limitations and avoid conclusions broader than the examination supports.

Likewise, retrospective visibility does not automatically establish negligence. The analysis remains fact-specific and requires careful separation of detection, characterisation, staging, breach and causation.
