## Introduction

Musculoskeletal imaging frequently produces disputes between an earlier normal or reassuring report and a later diagnosis.

A patient may undergo a radiograph after injury and later have MRI, CT, surgery or specialist review demonstrating significant pathology. That sequence can create an understandable assumption that the original examination must have been incorrectly reported.

The legal analysis is more exacting.

A later diagnosis does not determine what the earlier examination showed. Nor does the presence of an abnormality on MRI establish that it should have been visible on plain radiography.

The relevant questions are:

1. Was the original examination appropriate and technically adequate?
2. What did the images objectively demonstrate?
3. Was the report reasonably open to a competent reporter?
4. Should further imaging or clinical review have been advised?
5. Did any breach probably alter the patient's treatment or outcome?

## What Does “Unremarkable” Mean?

Terms such as “normal,” “unremarkable” and “no acute bony abnormality” are commonly used in musculoskeletal reporting.

They are not necessarily equivalent.

### Normal

“Normal” may be understood as indicating that no relevant abnormality is present.

It should be used carefully where the examination is limited, anatomy is incompletely demonstrated or chronic findings are present.

### Unremarkable

“Unremarkable” usually indicates that no finding of clinical significance has been identified within the scope of the examination.

The term does not mean that every visible structure is anatomically perfect. It may encompass minor variation or clinically insignificant change.

### No acute bony abnormality

This is narrower. It may be appropriate where the clinical question concerns fracture or dislocation and no acute osseous injury is identified.

It does not exclude:

- ligament injury;
- tendon injury;
- meniscal or labral pathology;
- marrow abnormality;
- cartilage injury;
- early infection;
- occult fracture; or
- another process poorly demonstrated by radiography.

The report should be read as a whole and in the context of the modality, clinical history and question asked.

## A Normal Report Does Not Require an Exhaustive Audit Trail

There is no general legal requirement for every normal musculoskeletal report to list each structure examined or document the reporter's complete search pattern.

A concise normal report may be entirely appropriate.

The question is whether the report:

- addresses the clinical indication;
- accurately reflects the acquired images;
- acknowledges material limitations;
- identifies significant abnormalities;
- avoids false reassurance beyond the capability of the examination; and
- recommends further investigation where reasonably indicated.

The brevity of a report does not establish negligence.

Conversely, a detailed report does not protect an interpretation that is not reasonably supported by the images.

## The Legal Framework

### Bolam

Under *Bolam v Friern Hospital Management Committee* [1957] 1 WLR 582, professional conduct is assessed by reference to a responsible body of appropriately skilled professional opinion.

In musculoskeletal reporting, this allows for reasonable differences in:

- recognition of subtle findings;
- interpretation of equivocal appearances;
- description of severity;
- selection of a differential diagnosis; and
- recommendations for further imaging.

The legal standard is reasonable competence, not retrospective perfection.

### Bolitho

Under *Bolitho v City and Hackney Health Authority* [1998] AC 232, supporting professional opinion must have a logical and defensible basis.

It is not enough for an expert to say that some radiologists would have issued the same report.

The opinion should explain:

- what the images showed;
- why the finding was or was not sufficiently conspicuous;
- how the clinical question affected interpretation;
- whether the views were adequate;
- whether the conclusion was consistent with the findings; and
- why further imaging was or was not required.

### Diagnostic interpretation

*Brady v Southend University Hospital NHS Foundation Trust* [2020] EWHC 158 (QB) confirms that *Bolam*, subject to *Bolitho*, applies to diagnostic judgment.

The court should distinguish:

1. the factual question of what the examination showed; and
2. the professional question of whether the report was reasonably open to a competent practitioner.

An eventual diagnosis different from the original opinion does not, without more, establish negligence.

## The Modality Question

The first task is to identify what the original modality could reasonably demonstrate.

### Plain radiography

Radiographs are usually effective for:

- displaced fracture;
- dislocation;
- alignment;
- advanced degenerative change;
- some focal bone lesions;
- joint effusion in certain anatomical regions;
- calcification; and
- some indirect signs of soft-tissue injury.

Radiographs are less sensitive for:

- non-displaced or occult fracture;
- marrow oedema;
- ligament and tendon injury;
- meniscal or labral lesions;
- cartilage injury;
- early infection;
- early inflammatory change; and
- subtle stress injury.

A later MRI diagnosis may therefore be entirely consistent with an earlier normal radiograph.

### CT

CT may demonstrate cortical fracture, complex anatomy and mineralised structures more clearly than radiography. It may still be limited for many soft-tissue and marrow processes.

### MRI

MRI may demonstrate pathology that was not visible or reasonably detectable on radiography.

The expert should not use the later MRI as proof that the same lesion must have been apparent on the earlier study. The proper comparison asks whether the earlier examination contained direct or indirect signs that a competent reporter should have recognised.

## Image Adequacy

Before assessing interpretation, the expert should determine whether the examination was technically capable of answering the clinical question.

Relevant matters include:

- anatomical coverage;
- projections obtained;
- positioning;
- exposure;
- movement;
- rotation;
- artefact;
- inclusion of the relevant joint;
- adequacy of weight-bearing views where indicated;
- availability of comparison imaging; and
- whether additional views were required.

If the examination was inadequate, responsibility may lie in:

- the referral;
- protocol selection;
- image acquisition;
- acceptance of inadequate images;
- failure to request additional views; or
- failure to communicate the limitation.

An interpretation dispute should not obscure a more fundamental acquisition problem.

## Clinical Information

The significance of an imaging appearance may depend on the information supplied.

Relevant details can include:

- mechanism of injury;
- precise site of pain;
- inability to bear weight;
- focal tenderness;
- locking or instability;
- history of malignancy;
- fever or inflammatory symptoms;
- previous surgery;
- duration of symptoms; and
- whether symptoms are acute, recurrent or progressive.

An inadequate referral does not excuse failure to identify a clear abnormality.

It may, however, affect:

- the appropriate examination;
- the focus of review;
- interpretation of equivocal findings;
- the relevance of chronic abnormalities; and
- the need for further imaging.

The medicolegal analysis should use the clinical information available to the original reporter, not a later reconstructed history.

## Demonstrability

“Visible” and “demonstrable” are sometimes used as though they answer the legal question. They do not carry a fixed legal definition.

A finding may be:

- visible only after annotation;
- visible with knowledge of the later diagnosis;
- equivocal but deserving mention;
- subtle yet sufficiently apparent to require action;
- obscured by anatomy or technical limitation; or
- absent from the original examination.

The expert should explain the actual degree of conspicuity rather than relying on labels.

Relevant features include:

- size;
- location;
- contrast against adjacent structures;
- visibility on more than one projection;
- cortical disruption;
- altered alignment;
- secondary soft-tissue signs;
- comparison with previous studies; and
- consistency with the clinical indication.

A finding does not become negligent merely because it can be pointed out later.

Equally, describing a finding as subtle does not provide a complete defence where its features should reasonably have prompted recognition or further investigation.

## Common Musculoskeletal Disputes

### Occult fracture

Some fractures are not visible on initial radiographs.

The review should consider:

- whether the fracture line was present;
- whether indirect signs were present;
- whether the views were adequate;
- whether the mechanism and examination suggested occult injury;
- whether immobilisation or further imaging was indicated; and
- whether repeat imaging would ordinarily have been advised.

### Subtle cortical or trabecular injury

A small cortical irregularity or trabecular change may be equivocal.

The issue is whether the appearance should have been:

- reported as a fracture;
- described as suspicious;
- correlated with focal tenderness;
- investigated with CT or MRI; or
- reasonably regarded as normal variation.

### Alignment abnormality

Malalignment may be the principal sign of ligamentous or developmental pathology.

The expert should consider positioning carefully. Apparent asymmetry may result from rotation, but genuine malalignment should not be dismissed without analysis.

### Bone lesion

A focal lesion may be missed, misclassified or omitted as incidental.

The relevant questions include:

- whether the lesion was sufficiently apparent;
- whether it had aggressive features;
- whether prior imaging demonstrated change;
- whether the report should have recommended further characterisation; and
- whether earlier recognition would probably have changed treatment or prognosis.

### Soft-tissue and internal derangement

Many soft-tissue abnormalities are not visible on radiography.

A normal radiograph does not exclude internal derangement. The reporting question is whether the radiograph showed an indirect sign or whether the clinical presentation required further assessment despite normal imaging.

### Spinal imaging

A normal radiograph does not exclude:

- disc prolapse;
- spinal cord or cauda equina compression;
- epidural disease;
- early infection;
- marrow infiltration; or
- many ligamentous injuries.

Where the suspected condition is not assessable on the examination performed, the report should not imply that it has been excluded.

## Language and Diagnostic Confidence

The report should use language proportionate to the evidence.

### Definite findings

Where the abnormality is clear, the report should state it directly.

### Equivocal findings

Where uncertainty remains, the report may appropriately use terms such as:

- possible;
- suspicious for;
- cannot exclude;
- equivocal;
- correlate with focal tenderness; or
- further imaging advised.

These expressions should assist management rather than merely transfer uncertainty to the referrer.

### Normal or reassuring findings

A normal conclusion should not exceed the capability of the examination.

For example:

> No acute osseous abnormality identified.

may be more precise than:

> Normal knee.

where the examination cannot assess menisci, cruciate ligaments, cartilage or marrow comprehensively.

## Recommendations for Further Imaging

A radiologist is not required to recommend MRI or CT after every normal radiograph.

Further imaging may be appropriate where:

- the clinical indication suggests important occult pathology;
- an equivocal abnormality requires clarification;
- the radiograph is technically limited;
- focal symptoms are unexplained;
- the result is discordant with the clinical presentation;
- a significant lesion requires characterisation; or
- established guidance supports additional investigation.

The adequacy of a recommendation should be assessed using the clinical information available at the time.

A later MRI showing pathology does not retrospectively prove that MRI should have been recommended earlier.

## Medicolegal Method

A structured expert review should separate the following stages.

### 1. Referral

- What symptoms and mechanism were recorded?
- What clinical question was asked?
- Was the selected modality appropriate?
- Was the examination appropriately prioritised?

### 2. Acquisition

- Were the necessary views obtained?
- Was the relevant anatomy included?
- Was image quality adequate?
- Were additional views required?

### 3. Interpretation

- What did the original images demonstrate?
- Was the disputed finding sufficiently conspicuous?
- Was the conclusion reasonably open to a competent reporter?
- Did the report answer the clinical question?

### 4. Recommendation and communication

- Was further imaging reasonably required?
- Were material limitations stated?
- Was an urgent or unexpected finding communicated appropriately?

### 5. Causation

- What would a competent report have said?
- What action would probably have followed?
- Would earlier investigation or treatment probably have changed the outcome?

## Perceptual and Cognitive Error

Radiological discrepancies are often described as perceptual or cognitive.

### Perceptual error

The abnormality is present but was not detected.

### Cognitive error

The abnormality was detected but incorrectly characterised or given inappropriate significance.

This distinction may assist analysis but does not determine negligence.

A perceptual miss is not automatically negligent. A cognitive error is not automatically defensible. Both require assessment against the standard of reasonable professional practice.

## Breach and Causation

Breach should be considered separately from causation.

Even where an abnormality should have been identified, the claimant must ordinarily prove that competent reporting would probably have altered the outcome.

The counterfactual should identify:

1. what the original report should have said;
2. whether further imaging or referral would have followed;
3. when that step would probably have occurred;
4. what it would probably have shown;
5. what treatment would then have been offered; and
6. whether earlier treatment would probably have reduced the injury or loss.

In musculoskeletal claims, earlier diagnosis may affect:

- immobilisation;
- weight-bearing;
- surgery;
- rehabilitation;
- return to work;
- deformity;
- arthritis;
- neurological injury; or
- duration of pain.

These consequences should be supported by appropriate orthopaedic, spinal, rheumatological or other specialist evidence where they extend beyond radiology.

## Evidence to Obtain

A disputed musculoskeletal report will commonly require:

- the original source images;
- all projections and series;
- the imaging request;
- the original report and any addenda;
- earlier and later imaging;
- emergency, clinic and examination records;
- operative findings where relevant;
- local imaging protocols;
- applicable professional guidance;
- records of communication;
- evidence of the treatment pathway; and
- relevant specialist evidence on causation and prognosis.

Screenshots and annotated images may assist explanation but should not replace review of the complete original examination.

## Practical Point for Radiologists

A defensible approach includes:

- reviewing the clinical indication;
- checking image adequacy before interpretation;
- using a consistent search pattern;
- comparing prior imaging where relevant;
- reporting significant incidental abnormalities;
- stating material limitations;
- matching diagnostic confidence to the appearances;
- recommending further imaging where reasonably indicated; and
- avoiding conclusions broader than the modality supports.

A report does not need to narrate every normal structure. It should, however, make clear what clinically important conclusion can reasonably be drawn from the examination.

## Practical Point for Expert Witnesses

The expert should avoid both hindsight criticism and automatic deference.

The opinion should explain:

- what the images showed;
- how conspicuous the finding was;
- whether the examination was adequate;
- how the clinical information affected interpretation;
- whether the original conclusion fell within responsible practice;
- whether supporting opinion is logically defensible; and
- what difference a competent report would probably have made.

The expert's role is not to ask whether the examination can now be read differently. It is to assist the court in deciding whether the original report fell below the standard reasonably expected at the time.

## Practical Point for Legal Professionals

The word “unremarkable” should not be treated as the breach.

The analysis should focus on:

- the scope and limitations of the examination;
- the actual images;
- the clinical question;
- the conspicuity and significance of the disputed finding;
- the range of responsible interpretation;
- the need for further imaging; and
- the causal consequences of any error.

A later abnormal MRI may establish the eventual diagnosis. It does not, without more, establish that an earlier radiograph was negligently reported.

## Conclusion

Musculoskeletal reporting claims should not be reduced to a comparison between an earlier normal report and a later abnormal examination.

The correct approach is to determine:

- whether the original examination was adequate;
- what the images objectively demonstrated;
- whether the report was reasonably open to a competent practitioner;
- whether the limitations of the modality were respected;
- whether further investigation was required; and
- whether any breach probably changed the outcome.

*Bolam* permits responsible differences in professional interpretation. *Bolitho* requires those opinions to be logically defensible.

The standard is reasonable professional practice in the circumstances, not retrospective perfection.
