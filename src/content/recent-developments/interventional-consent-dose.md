## Introduction

Interventional radiology blends diagnostic assessment with invasive procedures, making medicolegal review inherently multifaceted. Review typically examines whether the intervention was clinically indicated, reasonable alternatives were considered, the patient was adequately informed, sedation and monitoring were appropriate, radiation exposure was justified and optimised, the procedure was performed competently, complications were recognised and managed promptly, and the patient was informed appropriately when harm occurred. Crucially, a complication does not equate to negligence, nor does technical competence compensate for inadequate consent.

## Clinical Indication and Alternatives

The foundational question is whether the proposed intervention appropriately addressed the patient’s clinical problem. This requires evaluating the diagnosis, treatment objective, quality of pre-procedure imaging, and the patient’s comorbidities, physiological reserve, and urgency. Clinicians must weigh conservative, surgical, endoscopic, medical, or no-intervention alternatives against anticipated benefits and procedural risks. While the interventional radiologist is responsible for discussing procedure-specific alternatives, advice regarding surgical or oncological pathways should involve the relevant specialist teams.

## The Consent Discussion

Following _Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board_, clinicians must ensure patients are aware of material risks and reasonable alternatives. A risk is material if a reasonable person in the patient’s position would likely consider it significant, or if the clinician knows the specific patient would. Materiality transcends statistical probability; even small risks may be significant if consequences are severe or highly relevant to the patient’s circumstances, occupation, or values. Discussions should cover the procedure’s purpose, expected benefit, alternatives, options to defer, common and serious risks, patient-specific concerns, sedation arrangements, potential for technical failure or incomplete treatment, and the likelihood of requiring further intervention. Patients must be given adequate time and opportunity to ask questions.

## The Consent Record

A signed consent form evidences that a process occurred but does not replace a documented discussion. The clinical record should capture who led the discussion, when it took place, the indication, principal alternatives, material risks, patient-specific concerns, questions raised, educational materials provided, communication support used, and whether consent was revisited if circumstances changed. While phrases like “risks explained” offer limited protection in disputes, the absence of detailed notes does not automatically prove that no discussion occurred. Courts assess the totality of available evidence.

## Sedation and Analgesia

Sedation carries distinct risks separate from the radiological procedure itself. Medicolegal review examines the appropriateness of sedation relative to the procedure and patient, including pre-procedure assessment, fasting status, allergies, medication interactions, airway and cardiorespiratory risk, and the specific drugs, doses, routes, and timing administered. Monitoring, supplemental oxygen, personnel roles, availability of reversal agents and resuscitation equipment, and recovery observations must also be documented. Monitoring depth and frequency should match the patient, medication profile, sedation level, and setting. Contemporaneous recording is essential, as retrospective reconstruction from incomplete charts is often unreliable.

## Radiation Exposure

Radiation protection and informed consent are linked but distinct. Under the Ionising Radiation (Medical Exposure) Regulations 2017, exposures must be justified and optimised, with clear statutory roles for employers, referrers, practitioners, and operators. Procedure records should include fluoroscopy time, dose-area product, cumulative reference air kerma, acquisition runs, magnification/collimation settings, dose alerts, technical difficulties, dose-reduction measures, operator details, and any required post-procedure assessment. Fluoroscopy time alone is an incomplete proxy for patient dose and must be interpreted alongside other metrics, procedural complexity, and equipment factors.

## Radiation Risk and Consent

Radiation risk should be discussed when material to the individual’s decision-making process. Factors include expected exposure, likelihood of prolongation or repetition, pregnancy status, previous exposures, age, radiosensitive anatomy, potential for deterministic tissue reactions, and the availability of non-ionising alternatives. Detailed numerical stochastic risk explanations are rarely necessary; information must be proportionate, comprehensible, and directly relevant to the patient’s choice. Providing a technical dose figure without clinical context does not facilitate meaningful consent.

## Optimisation

Dose documentation captures what occurred but does not prove reasonableness. The review must assess whether exposure was kept as low as reasonably practicable while achieving the clinical goal. Complexity, urgency, operator experience, equipment capabilities, collimation, pulse/frame rates, magnification use, acquisition frequency, patient habitus, and procedural difficulty all contextualise exposure. While technically demanding procedures or haemodynamic instability may reasonably increase radiation use, complexity never absolves the duty to optimise and document technique.

## Unexpectedly High or Unintended Exposure

Accidental or significantly divergent exposures trigger investigation under local IR(ME)R protocols. Employers should preserve dose and equipment records, investigate the cause, assess clinical significance, consult medical physics, notify duty holders, inform the patient if clinically relevant, arrange follow-up, and report to the enforcing authority where required. Not every high-dose procedure constitutes an error; clinical purpose, anatomical complexity, and intended technique remain central to distinguishing acceptable practice from inadvertent harm.

## Procedural Complications

Complications may arise despite appropriate consent and competent technique. Liability hinges on whether the risk was material and disclosed, reasonable precautions were taken, the procedure was performed competently, the complication was recognised and managed promptly, adverse outcomes were documented, the patient was informed, and any delay caused additional harm. Recognised complications—such as haemorrhage, vascular injury, organ perforation, infection, contrast reactions, non-target embolisation, thromboembolism, pneumothorax, sedation-related respiratory compromise, or radiation-induced tissue injury—are not inherently negligent. Legal responsibility depends on the standard of care applied throughout and whether a breach causally worsened the outcome.

## Candour

When care causes or may cause harm, professional and organisational duties of candour require timely, factual, and proportionate communication. This includes explaining known facts, acknowledging uncertainty during ongoing investigations, offering an apology, outlining immediate and subsequent clinical responses, describing planned reviews, documenting the discussion, and providing updates as information evolves. An apology does not constitute legal liability, but candour should not be delayed pending final investigations. Clinicians must avoid speculation while ensuring transparent disclosure.

## Addenda and Corrections

Procedural and radiological records requiring correction must preserve the original entry for audit purposes. Amendments should clearly state what changed, why, who authorised it, the date and time, whether clinical management is affected, and whom was notified. Material corrections that could influence care should trigger active communication rather than relying on passive electronic discovery.

## Medicolegal Review

Expert analysis systematically separates consent adequacy, sedation management, radiation justification and optimisation, technical competence, complication recognition and treatment, candour compliance, and causation. Each domain is evaluated independently before synthesising whether alternative pathways would likely have altered the clinical outcome.

## Documents to Obtain

Comprehensive review typically requires referral documentation, clinical indication, pre-procedure imaging, procedure requests, justification records, clinic correspondence, patient information materials, signed consent forms, contemporaneous consent notes, procedural checklists, full procedure notes, nursing and physiological observation charts, sedation and medication records, fluoroscopy and acquisition logs, complete radiation dose reports, equipment dose alerts, post-procedure observations, intra- and post-procedural imaging, complication and escalation records, report addenda, incident reports, patient communication logs, and relevant local policies. While missing documentation can hinder reconstruction, it does not automatically equate to negligence. Clinical, imaging, and documentary evidence must be weighed together.

## Practical Conclusion

Interventional radiology claims rarely turn on isolated documents. A robust defence reconstructs the full pathway: indication, consent, sedation and monitoring, radiation justification and optimisation, technical performance, complication management, communication and candour, and causation. Keeping these elements distinct prevents two frequent errors: treating every complication as negligent and assuming a signed consent form exhausts the duty to inform.
