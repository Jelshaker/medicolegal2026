## Introduction

Cauda equina syndrome is a spinal emergency where delay can cause permanent bladder, bowel, sexual, sensory, or lower-limb dysfunction. Medicolegal claims typically involve a cascade of potential failures ranging from clinical triage and MRI prioritisation to image acquisition, interpretation, communication, and surgical timing. The radiologist’s role is defined and specific: to establish whether the examination was technically adequate, what the images objectively demonstrated, whether the report fell below acceptable professional standards, and whether a competent report would likely have altered the clinical timeline. Questions regarding surgical timing, urinary recovery, and long-term neurological prognosis generally require separate evidence from spinal surgery, neurology, or rehabilitation specialists, allowing the radiological opinion to remain focused and legally defensible.

## Clinical Chronology, Requests, and Protocol

Radiological analysis must be anchored in a precise chronological reconstruction documenting symptom onset, neurological progression, clinical examinations, bladder-scan results, and the sequential timeline of imaging requests, acquisitions, verifications, and communications. The original MRI request must be reviewed directly for red-flag symptoms, documented neurological deterioration, and assigned urgency. Radiologists or protocol vets should not downgrade an explicit suspicion of cauda equina syndrome without adequate clinical justification or strict adherence to local pathways. Before evaluating interpretation, the examination must be assessed for technical adequacy, including anatomical coverage, sequence selection, slice thickness, positioning, and artefact presence. While abbreviated protocols are clinically acceptable for initial emergency assessment, they must reliably address the urgent clinical question, and any technical limitations must be explicitly stated rather than concealed by a definitive negative report.

## Independent Review and Reporting Standards

The expert must independently review the complete original examination across all available planes. Sagittal images should be used to assess the craniocaudal extent of migrated disc material and canal compromise, while axial images clarify central or paracentral location, thecal sac displacement, and nerve root crowding. The report should clearly describe the level, cause, and severity of neural compression, residual cerebrospinal fluid, and any lateral recess involvement. Crucially, no single percentage of canal compromise independently determines cauda equina syndrome; imaging findings must always be interpreted alongside the clinical presentation. When significant compression is present, the conclusion must state the causative lesion, severity, and explicit need for urgent spinal surgical assessment, avoiding ambiguous phrases such as “clinical correlation advised” where a time-critical compressive lesion is evident.

## Interpretation, Communication, and the Chain of Events

Medico-legal analysis distinguishes between perceptual errors, where an abnormality is missed, and cognitive errors, where it is detected but mischaracterised or given insufficient significance. The central question remains whether a competent radiologist would have recognised the lesion given its actual conspicuity on the original images, independent of hindsight or later diagnoses. If a large extrusion is clearly demonstrated across appropriate sequences, a report stating no compressive lesion is present typically falls below professional standards. Furthermore, a correct report may still cause harm if time-critical findings are not communicated effectively. The review must verify verification timestamps, urgent alert generation, contact logs, acknowledgement receipts, and whether escalation occurred when initial contact failed. Passive electronic release is often insufficient for cauda equina compression; results must follow documented local escalation pathways to ensure the responsible spinal team receives timely, actionable information.

## Causation, Standards, and Judicial Evidence

Radiological breach and neurological causation are strictly separate. Liability requires constructing a specific counterfactual timeline: when the report should have been completed and verified, how and when it should have been communicated, and when spinal review and decompression would likely have followed. Even with a proven reporting error, the claimant must demonstrate that earlier intervention would probably have improved the neurological outcome. Medico-legal causation cannot be reduced to a rigid universal timeframe, as recovery depends on compression completeness, symptom duration, urinary retention status, rate of progression, and pre-existing spinal pathology. Broad statements about fixed recovery windows are clinically inadequate without case-specific support. The expert must distinguish direct imaging evidence from clinical inference, particularly when comparing serial MRIs, and avoid assuming that every documented delay caused the final disability. Professional standards should be judged against those applicable at the date of the event, ensuring historical care is assessed fairly within its contemporary context.

## Practical Guidance, Legal Distinctions, and Conclusion

Structured reporting templates can improve consistency for emergency lumbar MRIs, provided they do not encourage rote completion at the expense of independent interpretation. When instructing radiological experts, legal professionals should explicitly separate protocol adequacy, image findings, conspicuity, standard of care, communication timelines, and the limits of radiological causation evidence. Instructions must specify the date of the alleged breach, require application of contemporary professional standards, and explicitly limit the radiologist to radiological and administrative causation, leaving surgical and neurological outcome analysis to appropriately qualified specialists. This mirrors the distinction established in _Jarman v Brighton and Sussex University Hospitals NHS Trust_, which separated clinical triage from radiological interpretation. Ultimately, the radiologist’s contribution should remain precise: assess examination adequacy, identify compression, state what the report should have said, confirm urgent communication was required, establish the probable reporting counterfactual, and clearly identify which prognostic questions fall outside radiological expertise. A strong opinion does not assume that every delay caused the final disability but instead isolates the radiological breach and leaves surgical and neurological causation to the appropriate medical disciplines.
