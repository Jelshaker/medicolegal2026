AIM: United Kingdom medicolegal claims are rising. Many radiological allegations cite 
failure to report incidental findings, but these are often inconspicuous and asymptomatic and 
therefore difficult to detect. Nevertheless, claimants often support their allegations by citing 
the Royal College of Radiologists standards for the interpretation and reporting of imaging 
investigations. We examined our medicolegal practice to determine the proportion where 
allegations cited incidental pathology.
METHODS: Five medicolegal expert witnesses, working in abdominopelvic radiology, 
reviewed their medicolegal practice and determined the proportion of recent cases in which 
allegations of substandard reporting cited failure to diagnose incidental pathology. 
RESULTS: We identified 112 individual cases, comprising 176 imaging examinations; 99 
(56 %) were computed tomography and 36 (20 %) magnetic resonance imaging. One hundred 
forty-four (82 %) were reported in the National Health Service, 14 (8 %) outsourced, and 18 
(10 %) private practice. Medicolegal allegations cited failure to report incidental pathology in 
41 (37 %) of the 112 patients. The commonest allegation in this group was failure to identify 
tumour (23 of 41 patients, 56 %). Interpretation was judged as substandard in 12 (29 %) of 
these patients compared with 23 (32 %) of the 71 patients in whom allegations did not cite 
incidental findings.
CONCLUSION: A substantial proportion of medicolegal allegations in abdominal radiology 
cite failure to report incidental pathology. College standards should recognise that unantic-
ipated pathology is sometimes difficult to detect, especially without a clear steer from the 
referring clinician.

Introduction
United Kingdom medicolegal claims continue to rise 
steeply. A 2021 analysis found that the total annual cost of 
National Health Service (NHS) litigation had reached £3.6 
billion for 2018/2019. 1 Unfortunately, clinical radiology is 
well represented: of 10,900 NHS clinical negligence claims 
received for the year 2023/2024, 4.6 % were radiological, 
exactly the same total as for general medicine. 2 Because 
medical imaging is almost always archived, it is simple to 
establish whether an abnormality was present or not at the 
time of the original interpretation. Most radiological claims 
allege “missed” pathology. 3 The Royal College of Radiolo-
gists “standards for interpretation and reporting of imaging 
investigations” (Second Edition, March 2018) state that, 
“images should also be specifically and methodically 
interrogated”, and that this may disclose, “abnormal find-
ings both anticipated and unanticipated”. 4 Unanticipated 
pathology is often described as an “incidentaloma”, defined 
by a former college president as, “any finding not directly 
related to the primary purpose for which the imaging ex-
amination was undertaken”. 5 The authors all act as medi-
colegal expert witnesses, providing reports on liability for 
both claimant and defendant. We speculated that the 
proportion of cases based on failure to identify unantici-
pated incidentalomas was significant currently. Accord-
ingly, we performed a retrospective review of our cases in 
order to determine the proportion where allegations cited 
failure to report unanticipated pathology.
Materials and methods
Ethical permission was not required because our study 
was a retrospective case series review. No patients were 
involved directly and there was neither intervention nor 
change in patient management. Information was anony-
mised before sharing so that no patient identifiable data 
passed among study authors.
We performed a retrospective review of our medicolegal 
practice. All authors searched their personal medicolegal 
records for cases where the primary radiological allegation 
centred on substandard reporting of abdomino-pelvic 
radiology (which is the primary subspeciality interest of 
all authors). We searched retrospectively from April 2024 
until each individual author had accrued 30 consecutive 
cases or all available cases were exhausted, whichever 
occurred soonest. The limit of 30 cases was imposed, after 
discussion, to reduce individual burden since some experts 
had hundreds of individual cases. To avoid “double-
counting”, any mutual cases (ie, where one expert was 
acting for the claimant and the other for the defendant, for
the same case) were allocated to the expert called on behalf 
of the claimant. Knowledge of mutual cases was identified 
via prior case conference between the experts (occurring as 
part of the claim) and not via data sharing.
We then recorded the following data: claimants” age 
and sex at the time of the alleged radiological malpractice 
(at the time of index imaging since some claims involved 
multiple, sequential examinations); the imaging modality 
involved; the clinical indication for imaging stated on the 
imaging request; the specific allegation(s) made by the 
claimant; whether imaging was acquired in the NHS, out-
sourced by the NHS, or private sector; whether the expert 
was instructed by the defendant or claimant. The expert 
then noted whether the primary allegation involved an 
“unanticipated” finding or not and whether he considered 
the allegation of substandard reporting could be upheld. 
We defined an “unanticipated” finding as, “any finding not 
directly related to the primary purpose for which the im-
aging examination was undertaken”. 5 So, for example, 
alleged failure to report recurrent tumour in a patient 
imaged for colorectal cancer surveillance would not be 
considered incidental, whereas alleged failure to report a 
renal tumour in the same patient would. Similarly, if the 
patient had a history of colorectal cancer but this was not 
stated in the clinical details provided to the radiologist and 
the patient was being imaged for an alternative indication, 
then failure to report colorectal recurrence would count as 
an “incidental” case. Where multiple imaging episodes 
and/or modalities were performed in an individual patient, 
we included all instances where the expert was required to 
respond to a claimant’s allegation of substandard report-
ing. We excluded any case where there was no allegation of 
substandard reporting (eg, expert instructed to determine 
tumour stage, extent of injury etc.).
The first author assembled the data and categorised the 
clinical indication for each individual imaging investigation 
into broad groups, identified by thematic analysis.
The first author also categorised the primary medico-
legal allegation for each case into 11 individual categories, 
again identified by thematic analysis. Data were analysed 
using simple frequencies and proportions. Ninety-five 
percent confidence intervals around percentages were 
calculated using the binomial exact method (STATA 18.0, 
StataCorp).
Results

experience (years) was 3, 20, 10, 13, and 24 at the time of 
data collection (April 2024), respectively. They provided 10, 
12, 30, 30, and 30 individual cases, respectively, totalling

clinical indication for imaging (Table 1). Of these 41 pa-
tients (comprising 50 individual examinations), the orig-

(29 %, 95%CI: 16 %-46 %) patients (12 examinations). In the 
remaining 71 patients (63 %, 95%CI 54 %-72 %), the experts

incidental. Of these 71 patients (126 examinations), the 
original interpretation was judged to be substandard in 23 
(32 %, 95%CI: 22 %-45 %) patients (37 examinations). There 
was no significant difference between the proportion of 
allegations judged substandard when patients with inci-
dental and non-incidental findings were compared (29 % vs 
32 %, chi-squared: 0.12, P=.73).
Overall details of the 29 patients in whom interpretation 
of an incidental finding was judged reasonable (ie, not 
substandard) are given in Table 2. Examples of in-
terpretations of incidental pathology judged reasonable 
included failure to report a small pancreatic tumour in a 
patient undergoing dedicated small bowel MRI to search 
for Crohn’s disease; failure to report a small renal tumour 
in a patient undergoing lumbar MRI to investigate back 
pain; and several examples of failure to diagnose small 
colonic tumours (including flat polyps) in patients under-
going standard abdominopelvic CT, ie, where there had 
been no bowel preparation or gas insufflation.
Discussion
Radiological “incidentalomas” may be clinically signifi-
cant (eg, lung tumour) or insignificant (lung granuloma) 
and judged easily perceptible (large tumour) or not (small 
tumour), and have provoked medicolegal action for de-
cades. 6 Of course, error is inevitable for all practicing ra-
diologists, 7,8 so “missing” pathology does not automatically 
imply substandard (ie, negligent) practice. In the UK, the 
“Bolam test” is used to judge whether medical practice 
(which, for clinical radiology, usually means interpretation) 
is “reasonable” or “substandard”. Bolam considers whether 
a hypothetical body of responsible professionals (ie, 
“competent” and acting with “ordinary skill”) would act 
similarly. 9 This means that if the radiologist’s report was in 
accordance with the likely interpretation obtained from a 
body of radiologists, then it will not be found negligent. “In 
accordance” usually means majority opinion, although this 
may not apply in some areas where medical opinion di-
verges substantially. Expert medicolegal radiologists must 
therefore imagine themselves to be a properly trained 
“general” consultant radiologist faced with the clinical 
scenario at the time of the original interpretation and then 
come to a decision as to whether the issued report was 
substandard or not.
Hulson analysed 791 claims settled by the NHS Litiga-
tion Authority between 1995 and 2014, where the primary 
allegation focused on radiology. 10 Almost one-third of 
claims related to missed or delayed tumour diagnosis but 
data were not granular enough to determine whether these 
were incidental to the primary clinical question posed to 
the radiologist. We found that failure to report primary or 
recurrent tumour was the primary allegation in nearly half 
of our patients, with the tumour judged an incidental 
finding in more than half of these. Overall, our review 
found that nearly 40 % of allegations cited pathology that

While an expert witness will consider some incidenta-
lomas “unmissable” by any competent radiologist when 
applying the Bolam test, others will be judged missable by 
the, “majority of a body of consultant radiologists acting 
reasonably” (to use legal terminology), unless the radiol-
ogist is given a specific steer (and perhaps not even then). 
Perhaps unsurprisingly, two meta-analyses have concluded 
that diagnostic accuracy increases when radiologists are 
provided with relevant clinical details. 11,12 For example, we 
found several examples where the allegation cited failure 
to report tumours that were incidental to the clinical 
question being asked, often imaged using an inappropriate 
test (notably failure to report small colonic tumours from 
conventional abdominopelvic CT), and frequently where a 
prior history of malignancy was not supplied in the clinical 
details.
The 2018 Royal College standards make no distinction 
between an “unanticipated” finding sufficiently apparent 
that no reasonable radiologist should fail to report it, and 
an unanticipated finding that is so subtle it requires a 
dedicated and concerted search to unearth (such as that 
precipitated by a steer from the requesting clinician). It 
could be argued, therefore, that these college standards 
oblige radiologists (and reporting radiographers, sonogra-
phers, etc.) to detect and report all visible pathology, irre-
spective of the clinical details provided, and irrespective of 
conspicuity and clinical significance. This stance is one that 
all authors have encountered in their personal medicolegal 
practice, ie, the college guidelines are used by the claimant 
to support allegations of substandard practice, even when 
pathology is both incidental and inconspicuous.

the clinical question and/or symptoms could be interpreted 
as screening. We note that the UK National Screening

Committee spends considerable resource deciding who 
should be screened, what for, when, and with what test. 
While organised screening programmes seek to diagnose 
specific pathology in well-characterised groups of at-
tendees, modern medical imaging generates a plethora of 
data that encourages surreptitious screening for unantici-
pated, wide-ranging, asymptomatic pathology. This ap-
pears to burden radiologists specifically; for example, 
breast surgeons are not generally expected to detect inci-
dental colonic cancers.
We believe it is unreasonable and unrealistic to expect 
radiologists to unfailingly identify incidental, subtle pa-
thologies when the clinical details direct attention else-
where; we discovered that allegations related to incidental 
pathology in 37 % of our cases. We are certainly not arguing 
for lowered standards that excuse inadequate concentra-
tion: radiologists, quite rightly, should be expected to di-
agnose relevant pathology, both anticipated and 
unanticipated. However, the issue is at which point does 
unanticipated pathology become imperceptible without a 
specific steer? This is especially the case in a healthcare 
system under intolerable pressure and where radiologists 
are increasingly presented with less-specific referrals. 
Clear differential diagnosis is frequently replaced by 
symptomatology alone; “abdominal pain ? Cause”. It is the 
authors” experience that a large proportion of referrals 
now originate from non-medically qualified personnel, 
untrained in differential diagnosis.
The issue is exacerbated by the move in some legal cir-
cles to displace the Bolam test in favour of “pure diagnosis”, 
which conjectures that if pathology is at all visible, then 
failure to report it automatically represents substandard 
practice. 9 We believe this reflects a lack of understanding 
on the part of some legal professionals that radiological 
diagnosis is subjective and therefore subject to individual 
variation when the clinical importance of a potential 
finding is considered. All authors of this article have 
experienced “fishing trip” instructions from referring so-
licitors, where they have been asked to interpret all avail-
able imaging, with the hope of identifying any substandard 
practice in the absence of a prior, firm allegation. All au-
thors discourage this stance and take the position that 
experts require specific direction because, as we have 
outlined, clinical context and interpretation are 
inseparable.
Ultimately, we are not arguing for lower standards but 
believe that the spirit of the 2018 standards as written is 
being misinterpreted by some medicolegal experts (and/or 
solicitors/barristers/claimants). Indeed, all authors wish to 
emphasise the need for college standards and agree fully 
that it is right and proper that radiologists are regulated 
and held accountable. The general public also expect 
medical professions to be regulated. While the college may 
wish to avoid an explicit medicolegal stance, it is undeni-
able that the standards will be cited in court and, presently, 
they appear insufficiently granular regarding detection of 
anticipated vs unanticipated pathology. Without 
acknowledgement that incidentalomas pose specific
issues, some radiologists may adopt overly defensive 
reporting and/or devote undue amounts of time to inter-
pretation where the clinical question is clear and precise. 
Just as the Bolam test stipulates “reasonable” practice, 
standards should state what constitutes reasonable inter-
pretation in the light of the information and intent pro-
vided by referring clinicians.
Our review does have limitations. As a group of 
abdominal radiologists, our data are restricted to cases in 
this field. Accordingly, we cannot say if our findings 
generalise to other areas of imaging, although we would 
expect them to. The decision as to whether an allegation 
related to an incidental finding or not was made subjec-
tively, by the expert instructed originally to handle the 
case; we wished to avoid transfer of patient identifiable 
data, so authors only searched their own cases. Because 
there is often a legal instruction to destroy case details on 
settlement, we did not have data for some cases at the time 
of our audit. When preparing this article, we were aware 
that the 2018 college standards were being revised and 
these were published 13 subsequent to our article submis-
sion; one of our authors was a member of the guideline 
development group. The October 2025 standards state that 
the radiological report should, “allow incidental findings to 
be mentioned”, going on to state that, “there should be 
provision to ensure competent and holistic review of all 
relevant findings”, noting that, “responsibility for review-
ing nontarget areas lies with both the reporter and the 
employer”. 13 The new guidelines also state that, “inter-
pretation may be incorrect if referral details are deficient, 
absent, or misleading”, 13 which appears to potentially 
protect radiologists who miss subtle, incidental pathology 
without a specific steer. However, from a legal perspective, 
the standards to which radiologists are held are those in 
place at the time of the alleged substandard practice and 
not those most recently published.
In summary, a large proportion of medicolegal allega-
tions in abdominal radiology cite failure to report pathol-
ogy that is both inconspicuous and incidental to the clinical 
request, and many support their allegations by citing the 
2018 Royal College of Radiologists Standards for the inter-
pretation and reporting of imaging investigations. College 
standards should reflect the fact that unanticipated pa-
thology is sometimes difficult to detect, especially when 
the clinical details provided direct radiological attention 
elsewhere.
