## Introduction

Teleradiology enables imaging interpretation away from the acquisition site, supporting out-of-hours coverage, additional capacity, and specialist access. Remote location does not lower the standard of care. Safe remote reporting requires the reporter to have access to complete images, clinical information, prior examinations, reporting tools, and practicable communication routes. Concurrently, the healthcare provider and reporting organisation must maintain robust credentialing, workload controls, quality assurance, and incident management. The medicolegal question is never whether a report was generated remotely, but whether the entire reporting system was reasonable and whether any failure caused harm.

## Remote Reporting and Diagnostic Error

Published discrepancy rates in teleradiology vary widely based on modality, clinical setting, case complexity, subspecialist review, workload, and classification methods. A discrepancy does not equate to negligence, as two competent radiologists may reasonably differ on subtle or non-specific findings. Inferring teleradiology negligence from aggregate discrepancy rates is unsafe. Each case requires a focused analysis: examining the information and imaging available, assessing the adequacy of the reporting environment, determining if the interpretation was reasonable, evaluating communication practices, and establishing whether any identified failure probably altered the outcome.

## Clinical Information and Prior Imaging

Acute abdominal CT often requires correlation with symptoms, examination, laboratory results, operative history, and known disease. A request limited to “abdominal pain” may omit critical details about vascular risk, obstruction, perforation, immunosuppression, malignancy, or anticoagulation. While inadequate clinical information does not excuse missing a clear abnormality, it influences protocol selection, differential diagnosis, interpretation of equivocal findings, and the need for clarification or additional imaging. When information is insufficient, reporters must seek clarification or clearly state material limitations. Access to prior imaging is equally vital for establishing chronicity, identifying interval change, distinguishing postoperative anatomy, and preventing redundant recommendations. If priors are unavailable but known to exist, reporters should assess whether safe reporting is still possible, request retrieval, record the limitation, consider provisional reporting, and arrange subsequent addenda. Absence of prior imaging does not automatically make a report negligent; the issue is whether a competent reporter could reasonably provide the opinion without it and whether the limitation was managed appropriately.

## Technical Integration and Communication Pathways

Safe teleradiology demands full integration of pixel data, diagnostic workstations, referral access, reporting tools, and secure communication channels. Providers and remote organisations must clearly define work allocation, competence declarations, turnaround times, discrepancy review, critical-result communication, downtime procedures, and information governance. A written policy is inadequate if staff cannot practically execute it. Communication protocols must distinguish between routine electronic release and urgent notification. Time-critical, unexpected, or clinically significant findings require direct escalation beyond passive inbox delivery, with the method and urgency proportionate to the likely consequence of delay. Routine verification and electronic release remain sufficient for many reports, but findings that are immediately life-threatening, time-critical, suspicious for new malignancy, or likely to alter urgent management demand active notification. The reporter must use the agreed escalation route and document the action taken.

## Closed-Loop Communication and Shared Responsibilities

Effective reporting follows a closed-loop pathway: clear report creation, appropriate alert generation, confirmed receipt, documented acknowledgement, clinical review and action, and fail-safe escalation for unacknowledged items. A sent alert proves transmission but not comprehension or action, just as missing electronic acknowledgement does not preclude telephone or in-person communication. Responsibility is shared across the pathway. Reporters must issue accurate, appropriately prioritised reports, use agreed escalation routes, document significant communication, and raise safety concerns about flawed systems. The referring clinical team must maintain reliable reporting review, staff cover, alert acknowledgement, clinical action, and patient communication. Healthcare providers are responsible for defining stage-by-stage accountability, ensuring reliable digital and telephone communication, maintaining fail-safe processes, auditing acknowledgement and action, and integrating outsourced services into local governance. Teleradiology providers must verify credentials, allocate work appropriately, uphold technical and information-security standards, support report amendments, participate in incident review, and cooperate with healthcare organisations when concerns arise.

## Acute Abdominal Findings and Report Addenda

In acute abdominal imaging, several findings may demand urgent communication, including ruptured aneurysms, acute mesenteric occlusion, bowel ischaemia, perforated viscera, active haemorrhage, closed-loop obstruction, severe intra-abdominal sepsis, or misplaced devices. Not every abnormality requires a telephone call; notification must be proportionate to diagnostic certainty, treatment urgency, patient location, expectedness, and pathway reliability. Material report addenda constitute new communication events. When an addendum may alter management, reporters must determine if the original recipient remains responsible, whether the patient has changed teams, if direct notification is required, and how receipt will be confirmed. The record must preserve the original report while clearly documenting the amendment, author, timing, rationale, clinical significance, and individuals notified. Overwriting text without an audit trail obscures chronology and should be avoided.

## Duty of Candour and Discrepancy Review

Urgent-result communication and the duty of candour are distinct. The former secures timely clinical action; the latter addresses openness when care causes or may cause harm. The professional duty requires radiologists to inform patients or representatives, explain known facts, apologise, offer support, report internally, and cooperate with investigations, regardless of remote working. In England, the statutory duty applies to the regulated provider when notifiable safety incident criteria are met, requiring clear processes for assessment, communication, written follow-up, and documentation. Individual radiologists should not assume personal patient contact is always appropriate; coordination with the responsible clinical team and adherence to local procedures are essential. Furthermore, a later difference of opinion does not automatically trigger candour. Reviews must distinguish reasonable interpretive variation, minor discrepancies without clinical consequence, significant reporting errors, material addenda, and notifiable safety incidents. The duty should be assessed based on the specific facts and clinical consequences of the event rather than retrospective disagreement.

## Medicolegal Analysis and Evidence Collection

A medicolegal review of remote reporting examines the entire pathway: referral accuracy and clinical information, access to complete imaging and priors, interpretation reasonableness, notification methods and escalation, clinical action and follow-up, governance arrangements, and proximate causation. Evidence typically includes original DICOM data, imaging requests, acquisition records, PACS and RIS audit logs, report timestamps, alert and acknowledgement records, telephone logs, provider policies, service contracts, credentialing records, workload data, discrepancy reviews, incident reports, patient communication records, and clinical consequence evidence. Requests should remain proportionate, as past discrepancies do not prove negligence in a specific case. Practically, remote reporters should confirm complete study transfer, review referral information, access priors where required, state limitations clearly, avoid work outside competence, use agreed urgent-alert pathways, document communication contemporaneously, escalate unreachable recipients, treat significant addenda as requiring fresh notification, participate in audit and learning, and raise safety concerns about flawed systems. Providers must integrate remote reporting into local clinical governance, define responsibilities for priors and communication, ensure out-of-hours escalation works, test fail-safe and downtime procedures, monitor quality without treating discrepancy rates as negligence proxies, share incidents across organisational boundaries, and establish clear leadership for candour discussions.

## Conclusion

Teleradiology is a method of delivering radiological expertise, not a separate or lower standard of practice. Its safety depends on clinical information, prior imaging, technical integration, communication, acknowledgement, clinical action, and organisational governance. When harm follows a remote report, analysis must pinpoint exactly where the pathway failed rather than assuming remote location caused the error or confining review to the report text. The decisive questions remain whether the examination was reported reasonably, whether findings were communicated with appropriate urgency, whether organisations maintained a workable fail-safe system, whether patients and teams were treated openly when care went wrong, and whether competent reporting and communication would probably have changed the outcome.
