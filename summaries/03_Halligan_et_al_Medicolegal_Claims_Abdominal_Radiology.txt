SUMMARY: Medicolegal Implications of RCR Standards - Retrospective Review of
         Claims in Abdominal Radiology
SOURCE: Halligan S, Blunt D, Boone D, Higginson T, Plumb AA
JOURNAL: Clinical Radiology 92 (2026) 107162
PUBLICATION: Published by Elsevier Ltd on behalf of Royal College of Radiologists
ACCEPTED: 3 November 2025
================================================================================

OVERVIEW
--------
This retrospective study examined UK medicolegal claims in abdominal radiology
to determine the proportion where allegations cited failure to report incidental
pathology. Five expert medicolegal witnesses in abdominopelvic radiology reviewed
their practice, identifying whether failures to diagnose incidental findings were
cited in claims and whether interpretations were judged substandard.

CONTEXT
-------
UK medicolegal claims continue to rise steeply:
- NHS litigation total cost reached £3.6 billion for 2018/2019
- Of 10,900 NHS clinical negligence claims for 2023/2024, 4.6% were radiological
  (same total as general medicine)
- Medical imaging is almost always archived, making it easy to establish whether
  an abnormality was present at time of original interpretation
- Most radiological claims allege "missed" pathology
- The RCR 2018 Standards state images should be "specifically and methodically
  interrogated" and may disclose "abnormal findings both anticipated and unanticipated"
- "Incidentaloma" = any finding not directly related to the primary purpose of
  the imaging examination

METHODOLOGY
-----------
- Retrospective review of medicolegal practice by 5 expert witnesses
- Each author searched personal medicolegal records for substandard reporting
  cases in abdominopelvic radiology (primary subspecialty of all authors)
- Search period: April 2024 to 30 consecutive cases per author or all available
- 30-case limit imposed to reduce individual burden
- Mutual cases (where one expert acted for claimant, another for defendant)
  allocated to claimant expert only (identified via prior case conference)
- No ethical permission required (retrospective case series, no patient intervention)
- Information anonymised before sharing

DATA COLLECTED PER CASE:
- Claimant's age and sex at time of alleged radiological malpractice
- Imaging modality involved
- Clinical indication stated on imaging request
- Specific allegation(s) made by claimant
- Whether imaging acquired in NHS, outsourced NHS, or private sector
- Whether expert instructed by defendant or claimant
- Whether primary allegation involved an "unanticipated" finding
- Whether expert judged allegation of substandard reporting could be upheld

Definition: "Unanticipated" finding = "any finding not directly related to the
primary purpose for which the imaging examination was undertaken"
Example: Failure to report recurrent colorectal tumour in patient imaged for
colorectal surveillance = NOT incidental; failure to report renal tumour in same
patient = incidental

RESULTS
-------
112 individual patient cases were identified, comprising 176 imaging examinations:
- 99 (56%) computed tomography (CT, including CT colonography)
- 36 (20%) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI, including small bowel and MRCP)
- 16 (9%) ultrasound
- 13 (7%) plain film
- 12 (7%) fluoroscopic

Imaging acquired:
- 144 (82%) reported in NHS
- 14 (8%) outsourced
- 18 (10%, from 9 patients) private practice

Patient demographics:
- Median age: 53 years (range: 14-81)
- 37 male, 75 female
- Claimant to defendant expert split: 92 (82%) to 20 (18%)

Clinical indications for imaging:
- Abdominal symptoms: 99 examinations (56%)
- Postoperative complication: 21 (12%)
- Tumour recurrence/surveillance: 18 (10%)
- Sepsis: 17 (10%)
- Weight loss: 5 (3%)
- Miscellaneous: remainder

PRIMARY MEDICOLEGAL ALLEGATIONS:
- Primary tumour unreported: 40 cases (36%)
- Perforation unreported: 12 (11%)
- Obstruction unreported: 9 (8%)
- Recurrent tumour unreported: 7
- Sepsis unreported: 7
- Bariatric complication unreported: 6
- Cancer mis-staged: 3
- Thrombus unreported: 3
- Bowel ischaemia unreported: 2
- Stone unreported: 1
- Miscellaneous: 8

INCIDENTAL FINDING ALLEGATIONS:
- In 41 of 112 patients (37%, 95% CI: 28%-46%), the pathology was judged
  INCIDENTAL to the clinical indication
- Of these 41 patients (50 examinations):
  - Original interpretation judged SUBSTANDARD in 12 (29%, 95% CI: 16%-46%)
  - Original interpretation judged REASONABLE in 29 (71%)
- In 71 patients (63%) with NON-incidental findings (126 examinations):
  - Original interpretation judged SUBSTANDARD in 23 (32%, 95% CI: 22%-45%)

No significant difference between substandard rates for incidental vs non-incidental
findings: 29% vs 32% (chi-squared: 0.12, P=0.73)

6. Hepatic tumour missed on CT for suspected pancreatic cancer

8. Small bowel tumour missed on dedicated small bowel MRI
9. SMA thrombus missed on CT for suspected postoperative collection

11. Rectal cancer missed on MRI for suspected anal fistula

DISCUSSION - KEY LEGAL FRAMEWORK
--------------------------------
The "Bolam Test" in UK law:
- Used to judge whether medical practice (especially interpretation) is "reasonable"
  or "substandard"
- Considers whether a hypothetical body of responsible professionals (competent,
  acting with ordinary skill) would act similarly
- If radiologist's report accords with likely interpretation from body of radiologists,
  it will NOT be found negligent
- "In accordance" usually means majority opinion

"Pure Diagnosis" trend:
- Some legal circles moving to displace Bolam test in favour of "pure diagnosis"
- Conjecture: if pathology is at all visible, failure to report it automatically
  represents substandard practice
- Authors argue this reflects lack of understanding that radiological diagnosis is
  subjective and subject to individual variation

" Fishing Trip" Instructions:
- All authors experienced "fishing trip" instructions from referring solicitors
  (interpreting all available imaging hoping to find substandard practice)
- Authors discourage this stance - experts require specific direction
- Clinical context and interpretation are inseparable

INCIDENTAL FINDINGS CHALLENGE:
- Incidental findings may be clinically significant (lung tumour) or insignificant
  (lung granuloma)
- May be easily perceptible (large tumour) or not (small tumour)
- Error is inevitable for all practicing radiologists - "missing" pathology does not
  automatically imply negligent practice
- Meta-analyses confirm diagnostic accuracy increases when relevant clinical details
  are provided
- Frequently, prior history of malignancy was NOT supplied in clinical details

RCR 2018 STANDARDS CRITIQUE:
- Standards make NO distinction between:
  (a) Unanticipated finding sufficiently apparent that no reasonable radiologist
      should fail to report it
  (b) Unanticipated finding so subtle it requires dedicated, concerted search
- This may oblige radiologists to detect ALL visible pathology regardless of
  clinical details, conspicuity, or clinical significance
- This stance is used by claimants to support allegations even when pathology is
  both incidental and inconspicuous

SCREENING CONCERN:
- A requirement to identify incidental pathology could be interpreted as screening
- UK National Screening Committee carefully decides who is screened, what for, when,
  and with what test
- Modern imaging generates data encouraging "surreptitious screening" for
  asymptomatic pathology - burden falls specifically on radiologists
- Breast surgeons are not generally expected to detect incidental colonic cancers

REFERRAL QUALITY ISSUE:
- Healthcare system under "intolerable pressure"
- Radiologists increasingly presented with less-specific referrals
- Clear differential diagnosis frequently replaced by symptomatology alone
  (e.g., "abdominal pain ? Cause")
- Large proportion of referrals originate from non-medically qualified personnel
  untrained in differential diagnosis

THE AUTHORS' POSITION
---------------------
1. NOT arguing for lowered standards
2. Radiologists SHOULD diagnose relevant pathology (anticipated and unanticipated)
3. Question: at what point does unanticipated pathology become imperceptible
   without specific steer from referrer?
4. College standards should reflect that unanticipated pathology is sometimes
   difficult to detect, especially when clinical details direct attention elsewhere
5. Standards should state what constitutes "reasonable" interpretation in light of
   information and intent provided by referring clinicians
6. Without this acknowledgement, some radiologists may adopt overly defensive
   reporting or devote undue time to interpretation where clinical question is clear

LIMITATIONS
-----------
- Restricted to ABDOMINAL radiology - may not generalise to other imaging areas
- Classification of incidental vs non-incidental was SUBJECTIVE (by expert originally
  handling the case)
- Some cases lost due to legal instruction to destroy case details after settlement
- Study aware that 2018 RCR standards were being revised (2025 edition published
  after article submission)
- One author was member of 2025 guideline development group

2025 RCR STANDARDS RESPONSE:
The new (October 2025) standards:
- State report should "allow incidental findings to be mentioned"
- Note "there should be provision to ensure competent and holistic review of all
  relevant findings"
- Acknowledge "responsibility for reviewing non-target areas lies with both the
  reporter and the employer"
- State "interpretation may be incorrect if referral details are deficient, absent,
  or misleading"
- However, LEGALLY, radiologists are held to standards in place at time of alleged
  substandard practice, not subsequently published editions

CONCLUSION
----------
A large proportion of medicolegal allegations in abdominal radiology cite failure
to report pathology that is both inconspicuous and incidental to the clinical
request. Many claimants support allegations by citing the RCR Standards for
Interpretation and Reporting. College standards should reflect that unanticipated
pathology is sometimes difficult to detect, especially when clinical details
provided direct radiological attention elsewhere.

RELEVANCE FOR RADIOLOGICAL MEDILEGAL RESEARCH
This paper is CRITICAL for any medicolegal paper on radiology because it:
1. Provides empirical evidence that ~37% of medicolegal allegations in abdominal
   radiology concern INCIDENTAL findings
2. Shows that 29% of incidental finding cases were judged substandard - meaning
   most are NOT negligent
3. Demonstrates the tension between RCR reporting standards and legal expectations
4. Challenges the "pure diagnosis" approach to negligence determination
5. Highlights the problem of inadequate clinical detail in referrals
6. Addresses the "screening" implications of universal incidentaloma detection
7. Provides specific case examples that illustrate the boundary between reasonable
   and substandard interpretation
8. Raises important questions about the Bolam test's application to radiological
   interpretation
The paper suggests that the 2025 RCR Standards represent an attempt to address
these concerns by acknowledging shared responsibility for non-target area review
and the impact of poor referral information.
