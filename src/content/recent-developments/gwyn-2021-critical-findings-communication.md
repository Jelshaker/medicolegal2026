## Introduction

A radiology report has limited clinical value if a significant finding does not reach the person responsible for acting on it.

Most reports can be communicated through the ordinary electronic reporting pathway. Some findings, however, are sufficiently urgent, unexpected or clinically important to require additional notification.

The appropriate response depends on the finding, the clinical circumstances and the time within which action is required. The central principle is that communication should be proportionate to clinical risk and supported by a system that reduces the chance of important results being overlooked.

## A System-Wide Responsibility

Failures in imaging-result communication are rarely attributable to one person alone.

The reporting pathway may involve:

1. the clinician requesting the examination;
2. the team acquiring the images;
3. the radiologist or other authorised reporter;
4. the electronic reporting and alert systems;
5. the referring or responsible clinical team;
6. administrative and booking services; and
7. the clinician who communicates the result and plan to the patient.

A safe system should define where responsibility passes between these stages and what happens when the expected recipient is unavailable, the patient has moved between teams or acknowledgement is not received.

## The Reporter’s Responsibility

The reporter should determine whether the routine report is sufficient or whether additional communication is required.

Relevant factors include:

- the severity of the finding;
- the speed with which harm may occur;
- whether the finding was expected;
- whether the patient remains in the hospital or has been discharged;
- whether the responsible clinician can be identified;
- the reliability of the usual reporting pathway; and
- the consequences of delayed action.

Where additional notification is required, the reporter should use the locally agreed escalation route and record the communication.

The record should ordinarily identify:

- the finding communicated;
- the date and time;
- the method of communication;
- the recipient; and
- any advice or recommended action conveyed.

The radiologist is not ordinarily responsible for managing the patient beyond the scope of their role. The reporter is, however, responsible for communicating a time-critical finding with sufficient clarity and urgency.

## The Clinical Team’s Responsibility

The referring or otherwise responsible clinical team should have arrangements for:

- reviewing imaging reports;
- acknowledging significant alerts;
- acting on recommendations;
- arranging further investigation or treatment;
- communicating relevant results to the patient; and
- ensuring continuity when responsibility transfers between teams.

An alert does not by itself complete the pathway. It must reach an appropriate recipient and lead to clinical consideration.

The responsible clinician may reasonably decide not to follow a radiological recommendation, but that decision should be clinically informed and recorded.

## The Provider’s Responsibility

Healthcare providers should maintain documented procedures for significant imaging findings.

These should address:

- which findings require an alert;
- expected communication methods;
- levels of urgency;
- out-of-hours arrangements;
- unavailable or unidentified referrers;
- acknowledgement of alerts;
- escalation where acknowledgement is absent;
- significant report addenda;
- communication with patients;
- audit and incident review; and
- responsibilities when care crosses organisational boundaries.

Electronic systems can support safe communication but do not remove the need for clear human responsibility.

A notification system should not assume that sending an automated message proves that the result has been read, understood or acted upon.

## Urgent and Unexpected Findings

Not every abnormality requires direct telephone communication.

The method should reflect the likely consequence of delay.

### Immediately time-critical findings

Examples may include an unstable intracranial haemorrhage, tension pneumothorax, major vascular occlusion or other abnormality requiring immediate clinical action.

These findings will usually require direct and prompt communication to an appropriate clinician.

### Significant findings requiring urgent follow-up

A suspected new malignancy or other serious unexpected abnormality may not require action within minutes, but it should not depend solely on passive review of an electronic inbox.

The local system should ensure that the result is brought to the attention of a responsible team within an appropriate period.

### Important report addenda

Where a report is materially amended after release, the reporter should consider whether the change affects management.

A clinically significant addendum may require active notification rather than reliance on the amended report being discovered later.

## Closed-Loop Communication

The expression “closed loop” describes a pathway in which the result is:

1. issued;
2. directed to an appropriate recipient;
3. acknowledged;
4. clinically reviewed; and
5. acted upon where necessary.

A fully effective system also identifies cases where the loop remains incomplete.

The precise arrangements will vary between organisations. The essential requirement is that the pathway should not rely on an untested assumption that somebody else has seen and acted on the result.

## Medicolegal Analysis

A claim arising from failed communication should identify the precise stage at which the pathway broke down.

The relevant questions may include:

- Was the finding correctly identified?
- Did it require additional communication?
- Was the correct escalation procedure followed?
- Was an appropriate person contacted?
- Was communication documented?
- Was acknowledgement required or obtained?
- Did the recipient act reasonably?
- Did the provider maintain an adequate fail-safe system?
- Would timely action probably have changed the outcome?

The communication failure and its clinical consequences should be analysed separately.

Even where the communication system was deficient, the claimant must ordinarily establish that earlier receipt and action would probably have avoided or reduced the injury.

## Evidence to Obtain

These cases often depend less on reinterpreting the images than on reconstructing the communication pathway.

Relevant material may include:

- the original report and any addenda;
- report verification and release times;
- electronic alert records;
- acknowledgement logs;
- telephone or switchboard records;
- secure-message and email records;
- electronic health-record access logs;
- referral and clinic records;
- local escalation policies;
- staff rotas and inbox-cover arrangements;
- incident reports and investigation findings; and
- evidence of what treatment would have followed earlier communication.

The absence of an electronic acknowledgement does not necessarily prove that no communication occurred. Equally, the presence of a sent alert does not necessarily prove that the result reached a clinician able to act.

## Practical Principles

### For radiologists

- Use the agreed escalation pathway.
- Communicate with urgency proportionate to the finding.
- Identify an appropriate recipient rather than relying on an unattended destination.
- Record significant communication clearly.
- Escalate when the usual recipient cannot be reached.
- Treat a material addendum as a new communication event where management may change.

### For clinical teams

- Maintain reliable arrangements for reviewing reports and alerts.
- Provide cover for absent staff and unattended inboxes.
- Record the clinical response to significant findings.
- Ensure that responsibility is explicit when care transfers between teams.
- Communicate the result and plan to the patient where appropriate.

### For healthcare providers

- Define responsibilities across the full reporting pathway.
- Use acknowledgement and fail-safe processes where proportionate.
- Audit whether significant findings are acted upon.
- Review failures as system events rather than assuming that issuing the report completed the duty.

## Conclusion

Safe imaging communication is not achieved simply by placing a correct report on an electronic system.

The appropriate legal and professional analysis considers the entire pathway: identification, notification, receipt, acknowledgement, clinical review and action.

Responsibility may rest with the individual reporter, the responsible clinical team, the provider’s systems or a combination of all three. The decisive question is where the pathway failed and whether timely communication and action would probably have changed the outcome.
