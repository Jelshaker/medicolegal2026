**Spellman v Portsmouth Hospitals University NHS Trust**

[2024] EWHC 2011 (KB)

## Issue

standard of interpretation in a "pure diagnosis" radiology claim — when disagreement between radiologists is negligence and when it is not.

## Facts

after a fall with severe back pain, the claimant alleged the emergency MRI showed acute cauda equina syndrome (CES) compression which the reporting radiologist missed, reporting only mild spinal stenosis. Sweeting J heard competing expert evidence and preferred the defendant's evidence that acute compression was factually absent from the scan as acquired.

## Principle

subjective variance in reporting is acceptable where the evaluation is logically supported (*Bolam* as qualified by *Bolitho v City and Hackney HA [1998] AC 232*). The court rejected the "pure diagnosis" invitation to decide negligence simply by preferring one expert's reading of the images: the question remains whether the defendant's interpretation was supported by a responsible, logical body of radiological opinion at the time. Here it was, and the claim was dismissed. Contrast *Penney* ([case-law index 02](/radiology-legal-cases#screening)) and *EXP v Barker* ([case-law index 07](/radiology-legal-cases#missed-aneurysm)): where an abnormality is demonstrably present and plainly visible, Bolam does not save a perceptual miss. *Halligan et al. (Clin Radiol 2026)* — reviewed from the *medicolegalcourtcase.pdf* extract — shows why this boundary matters in practice: most challenged interpretations (71% of incidental, 68% of non-incidental in that series) were judged reasonable. The Scottish test is identical: *Hunter v Hanley 1955 SC 200* (the Scots origin of *Bolam*) and *Bolitho* apply in the Court of Session as in England and Wales. MDU analysis (*article-1789575458816.pdf* extract: 200 claims, ~75% defended) confirms that reasonable interpretation in light of the clinical information provided remains defensible.

## Why it matters — radiology

emergency spinal MRI is the highest-stakes "pure diagnosis" examination in general radiology. Spellman confirms that a careful, reasoned negative — documented sequences, levels examined, and why criteria for acute compression were not met — is defensible even when a later expert disagrees.

## For consultant radiologists

show your reasoning on the face of the report. "No acute compression" should be supported by what was assessed (conus, cauda equina, canal, discs, haematoma) so a Bolitho challenge has something logical to test. Escalate clinically where the picture is borderline rather than hedging in the text alone.

## For legal professionals

a battle of experts on the images is not enough — the claimant must show no responsible, logical body of radiologists would have reported as the defendant did. Ask experts to address the scan *as acquired* and to separate hindsight from contemporaneous reasonableness.
