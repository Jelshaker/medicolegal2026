**Shaheen and another v Daish**  
**[2025] EWHC 3056 (KB)**

Judgment was given by Christopher Kennedy KC, sitting as a Deputy Judge of the High Court, on 28 November 2025.

## Scope of the Judgment

The claim was brought by Mrs Amina Shaheen and Mr Mudassar Ahmed as executors of the estate of Mr Ajaz Ahmed and on behalf of his dependants.

Mr Ahmed died from lung cancer on 23 February 2023 at the age of 49.

The judgment concerned three preliminary issues:

1. whether the defendant GP told Mr Ahmed that a chest radiograph had been requested and that he needed to attend a walk-in radiology department;
2. whether Mr Ahmed would have attended if he had been properly informed; and
3. whether Mr Ahmed was contributorily negligent.

The judgment did not determine whether earlier cancer treatment would probably have altered the disease course. It did not determine quantum.

## Facts

Mr Ahmed attended an urgent general-practice appointment with Dr Joanna Daish on 11 February 2019.

He reported worsening breathlessness over several months following a lower respiratory tract infection.

Dr Daish examined him, diagnosed an exacerbation of asthma and prescribed treatment. She also generated an electronic request for a chest radiograph using the Integrated Clinical Environment system.

Under the local arrangements, the electronic request did not create an appointment. The patient had to attend a walk-in radiology department before the radiograph would be performed.

There was no automatic follow-up to the GP or patient if the requested examination did not take place.

Mr Ahmed did not attend for the radiograph.

The chest radiograph request appeared in the electronic record, but the consultation note did not record that Mr Ahmed had been told:

- that the investigation had been requested;
- why it was required;
- where he needed to attend; or
- what action he needed to take.

Dr Daish had no specific recollection of the consultation. Her evidence was that her usual practice was to tell patients when imaging had been requested and explain the process.

The electronic evidence indicated that the chest radiograph request was completed after Mr Ahmed had left the consulting room.

Mr Ahmed's lung cancer was diagnosed after a chest radiograph in January 2020.

## Agreed Medical Position

The parties agreed that, if the requested chest radiograph had been performed in 2019, it would have shown an abnormality.

That abnormality would have led to CT imaging and treatment for cancer.

The parties did not agree that the earlier investigation and treatment would probably have altered the progression or outcome of the disease.

That causation issue was outside the preliminary judgment.

## The Claimants’ Case

The claimants alleged that Dr Daish failed to tell Mr Ahmed:

- that a chest radiograph had been requested;
- that he needed to attend a walk-in radiology department;
- how to obtain the examination; and
- the clinical importance of doing so.

They contended that Mr Ahmed would have attended if he had received the necessary information.

## The Defendant’s Case

Dr Daish accepted that failure to tell Mr Ahmed about the request and walk-in process would have amounted to breach of duty.

She denied that the failure had occurred.

Although she could not recall the individual consultation, she relied on her usual practice of informing patients about requested investigations and explaining what they needed to do.

The defendant also alleged contributory negligence based on Mr Ahmed's failure to attend later follow-up appointments and take further steps concerning his health.

## Decision

The court found for the claimants on all three preliminary issues.

It held, on the balance of probabilities, that Dr Daish had not told Mr Ahmed that:

- a chest radiograph had been requested; or
- he needed to attend the walk-in radiology department for it to be performed.

The court also found that Mr Ahmed would probably have attended for the radiograph if he had been properly informed.

The allegation of contributory negligence was rejected.

## Contemporaneous Records and Usual Practice

The judgment is significant for its treatment of a silent contemporaneous record and evidence of usual practice.

The absence of a note was not treated as automatic proof that no discussion occurred.

It was, however, an important part of the evidence when considered alongside:

- the detailed recording of other aspects of the consultation;
- the absence of any recorded discussion about the radiograph;
- the timing of the electronic request;
- evidence that Mr Ahmed had already left when parts of the clinical plan were completed;
- entries made by other clinicians recording discussions about investigations;
- Mr Ahmed's concern about his health; and
- his previous conduct in following advice about investigations.

The court accepted that Dr Daish was a caring and competent doctor. That did not determine what happened during this particular consultation.

Evidence of a clinician's usual practice may be relevant, but it does not necessarily outweigh the contemporaneous record and the full factual context.

## Principle

Where an investigation will occur only if the patient takes a further step, the clinician should communicate enough information to enable that step to be taken.

Depending on the circumstances, this may include:

- that the investigation has been requested;
- why it is required;
- where the patient must attend;
- whether an appointment will be issued;
- the expected timescale;
- what the patient should do if the examination does not occur; and
- any urgency or serious diagnostic concern that the patient reasonably needs to understand.

The judgment does not establish a universal requirement to discuss every possible diagnosis whenever imaging is requested.

Its central point is narrower: a patient cannot reasonably be expected to attend an investigation if they have not been told that it has been requested or what they need to do.

## Contributory Negligence

The defendant bore the burden of proving contributory negligence.

The court recognised that findings of contributory negligence in clinical-negligence claims are comparatively unusual.

Patient non-attendance does not automatically amount to contributory negligence.

The relevant questions include:

- what advice was given;
- whether the patient understood it;
- whether the importance of attendance was communicated;
- whether the patient knew what action to take;
- whether the patient appreciated the risk of failing to act;
- whether there was a reasonable explanation for non-attendance; and
- whether the alleged failure contributed to the injury.

In earlier cases where contributory negligence had been established, there was evidence that the patient understood the medical advice and significance of failing to follow it.

That evidence was absent in this case.

The court did not accept that Mr Ahmed's later missed appointments proved that he would have ignored a clearly explained chest radiograph request.

## Counterfactual Attendance

The question whether Mr Ahmed would have attended if informed was a factual causation issue distinct from medical causation.

The court considered evidence about:

- his concern about his symptoms;
- his previous engagement with healthcare;
- his conduct concerning other requested tests;
- his response to medical advice; and
- the practical accessibility of the walk-in service.

It concluded that he would probably have attended.

This finding does not resolve whether the resulting earlier cancer treatment would probably have changed the eventual outcome.

## Radiological Significance

This was not a case about negligent image acquisition or interpretation.

No chest radiograph was performed in response to the 2019 request. There was therefore:

- no image to interpret;
- no radiology report;
- no missed abnormality by a radiologist; and
- no critical imaging result requiring acknowledgement.

The failure occurred before imaging.

The case nevertheless has relevance to imaging pathways because it demonstrates the risk created when an electronic request is treated as complete even though the patient remains responsible for initiating the next step.

A request can exist electronically without producing:

- an appointment;
- attendance;
- image acquisition;
- a report; or
- a clinical result.

## Referral Is Not Completion

The imaging pathway may contain several separate stages:

1. the clinician decides that imaging is required;
2. the request is created;
3. the patient is informed;
4. the request is received and triaged;
5. an appointment is issued or walk-in attendance occurs;
6. the examination is performed;
7. the examination is reported;
8. the result is reviewed; and
9. clinical action follows.

A system may fail at any of these stages.

The legal responsibility attached to each stage depends on:

- the local process;
- what each participant knew;
- the responsibilities assigned by policy;
- the urgency of the examination;
- and the practical ability to identify and correct failure.

## Unperformed Requests and Fail-Safe Systems

The judgment recorded that the local walk-in request system generated no automatic follow-up if the patient did not attend.

That feature formed part of the factual background. The court did not decide that the radiology provider had breached a legal duty by failing to track the request.

The case should therefore not be cited as authority for a general proposition that radiology departments must monitor every unperformed request.

It does, however, illustrate why providers should define:

- who is responsible for informing the patient;
- whether the request creates an appointment;
- how urgent requests are monitored;
- whether non-attendance is visible to the referrer;
- whether suspected-cancer referrals require additional tracking;
- what happens when an examination is not completed;
- and who closes the loop.

The appropriate system will depend on the type and urgency of the examination, the referral pathway and available technology.

## Distinguishing Three Different Failures

### Unperformed investigation

The requested examination never takes place.

Relevant evidence includes:

- the request;
- patient instructions;
- booking records;
- attendance records;
- reminder systems;
- cancellation records;
- and follow-up arrangements.

### Unreported investigation

The examination is performed but no report is produced within the required period.

Relevant evidence includes:

- acquisition timestamps;
- worklist allocation;
- reporting status;
- escalation;
- backlog arrangements;
- and report verification.

### Unactioned result

The examination is reported, but the significant result is not reviewed or acted upon.

Relevant evidence includes:

- report release;
- alerts;
- acknowledgement;
- electronic access logs;
- clinical records;
- follow-up;
- and fail-safe escalation.

*Shaheen* concerned the first category. It should not be conflated with a failure to communicate an imaging result.

## Practical Point for Referring Clinicians

Where the patient must arrange or initiate the investigation, the record should state:

- that the investigation was requested;
- the reason for it;
- the action required from the patient;
- where the patient should attend;
- the expected timescale;
- the degree of urgency;
- what happens next; and
- what the patient should do if the investigation does not take place.

The detail required will vary with the seriousness and urgency of the clinical concern.

The record need not reproduce every word of the discussion. It should be sufficient to show the essential advice and plan.

## Practical Point for Imaging Providers

Imaging providers should make the referral process clear to referrers and patients.

Relevant safeguards may include:

- confirmation that the request has been received;
- clear distinction between appointment-based and walk-in services;
- accessible patient instructions;
- visibility of request status;
- notification of rejected or incomplete requests;
- monitoring of urgent referrals;
- arrangements for non-attendance;
- and communication back to the referrer where a significant pathway remains incomplete.

The provider's responsibility should not be inferred from this judgment alone. It should be assessed using the actual local pathway, policy and contractual or organisational arrangements.

## Practical Point for Radiologists

Most radiologists will not see a request for an examination that never enters a reporting worklist.

The issue is therefore principally one of referral and pathway governance rather than image interpretation.

Radiologists involved in service leadership may nevertheless contribute by ensuring that:

- urgent pathways are clearly defined;
- responsibilities are allocated;
- rejected or downgraded requests are communicated;
- administrative systems identify unresolved urgent referrals;
- and imaging governance distinguishes unperformed, unreported and unactioned examinations.

## Practical Point for Legal Professionals

The correct disclosure depends on the alleged stage of failure.

For an unperformed walk-in investigation, relevant material may include:

- the consultation record;
- the original electronic request;
- request creation and submission timestamps;
- the system workflow;
- patient information;
- appointment or walk-in instructions;
- text messages and letters;
- subsequent attendance records;
- follow-up appointments;
- non-attendance policies;
- and evidence of the patient's previous engagement with medical advice.

PACS reporting logs and report-acknowledgement records will usually be irrelevant where no examination took place.

## Medical Causation

The preliminary judgment did not resolve whether earlier diagnosis would probably have changed the outcome.

That analysis would require a staged counterfactual:

1. When would Mr Ahmed probably have attended?
2. What would the chest radiograph have shown?
3. When would CT have occurred?
4. What stage would the cancer probably have been?
5. What treatment would have been offered?
6. Would that treatment probably have altered survival or other loss?

Radiological evidence may assist with:

- what the earlier radiograph would probably have demonstrated;
- the likely CT findings;
- imaging stage;
- disease progression;
- and the timing of diagnosis.

Oncological treatment, prognosis and survival ordinarily require specialist cancer evidence.

## Relationship With Darnley

*Darnley v Croydon Health Services NHS Trust* [2018] UKSC 50 concerned inaccurate information given to a patient about the likely waiting time in an emergency department.

*Shaheen* concerned an alleged failure to provide information about an investigation and the steps needed to obtain it.

Both cases demonstrate that non-clinical or pathway information may affect whether a patient receives timely care.

They should not be treated as establishing the same duty or factual test. Each depends on the information given, the patient's response and the causal consequences.

## Evidence of Subsequent Non-Attendance

Later missed appointments may be relevant to the counterfactual question but are not decisive.

Their significance depends on:

- whether the patient knew about them;
- how they were communicated;
- the stated purpose;
- perceived urgency;
- practical barriers;
- the patient's health and circumstances;
- and the patient's broader pattern of engagement.

A court should not infer from one missed appointment that the patient would have ignored a clearly explained investigation for possible serious disease.

## Conclusion

*Shaheen* is not a radiological interpretation case.

It is authority on communication of an imaging request, factual counterfactual attendance and contributory negligence.

The principal lessons are:

- an electronic request does not ensure that imaging will occur;
- a patient should be told what investigation has been requested and what action is required;
- contemporaneous documentation may be important where later recollection is absent;
- usual-practice evidence is relevant but not conclusive;
- non-attendance is not automatically contributory negligence;
- the patient must have understood the relevant advice and significance before failure to act can fairly be criticised; and
- medical causation remains separate from breach and counterfactual attendance.

The case exposes a wider systems question about unperformed imaging requests, but it does not decide that radiology departments must track every referral to completion.
