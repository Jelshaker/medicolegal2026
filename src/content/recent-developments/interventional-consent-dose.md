## Introduction

Interventional radiology combines diagnostic judgment with invasive procedural care. A medicolegal review may therefore involve several distinct questions:

- whether the procedure was clinically indicated;
- whether reasonable alternatives were considered;
- whether the patient was adequately informed;
- whether sedation and monitoring were appropriate;
- whether radiation exposure was justified and optimised;
- whether the procedure was technically competent;
- whether a complication was recognised and managed promptly; and
- whether the patient was informed appropriately when something went wrong.

These questions should be analysed separately. A complication does not by itself establish negligence, and a technically competent procedure does not cure inadequate consent.

## Clinical Indication and Alternatives

The first question is whether the proposed intervention was appropriate for the patient and clinical problem.

Relevant considerations may include:

- the diagnosis and treatment objective;
- the quality and sufficiency of the pre-procedure imaging;
- conservative management;
- surgical, endoscopic or medical alternatives;
- the option of no immediate intervention;
- the patient's comorbidities and physiological reserve;
- the urgency of treatment; and
- the balance between anticipated benefit and procedural risk.

The range of reasonable alternatives depends on the individual case. The interventional radiologist is responsible for discussing matters within their expertise, while advice about surgery, oncology or other treatment pathways may require input from the relevant clinical team.

## The Consent Discussion

Under *Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board* [2015] UKSC 11, the clinician must take reasonable care to ensure that the patient is aware of material risks associated with the recommended treatment and of any reasonable alternatives.

A risk is material where:

- a reasonable person in the patient's position would probably attach significance to it; or
- the clinician should reasonably know that the particular patient would attach significance to it.

Materiality is not determined by percentage alone. A numerically small risk may be important where the potential consequence is grave or particularly relevant to the patient's circumstances, occupation, values or expressed concerns.

The discussion should ordinarily address:

- the purpose of the procedure;
- the expected benefit;
- reasonable alternatives;
- the option of no intervention where relevant;
- common complications;
- serious complications, even if uncommon, where material;
- patient-specific risks;
- sedation or anaesthetic arrangements;
- the possibility of technical failure or incomplete treatment;
- the potential need for further intervention; and
- relevant limitations in the available evidence.

The patient should have an opportunity to ask questions and sufficient time to consider the information where the clinical circumstances permit.

## The Consent Record

A signed consent form is evidence that a process occurred, but it is not the whole consent process.

The contemporaneous record should make clear:

- who discussed the procedure;
- when the discussion occurred;
- the indication;
- the principal alternatives;
- the material risks discussed;
- any patient-specific concerns;
- questions raised by the patient;
- information leaflets or other material supplied;
- whether an interpreter or communication support was required; and
- whether consent was revisited where the procedure or circumstances changed.

Generic phrases such as “risks explained” may provide limited assistance where the content of the discussion is disputed.

Equally, absence of a detailed note does not automatically prove that no discussion occurred. The court considers the available evidence as a whole.

## Sedation and Analgesia

Sedation introduces risks separate from those inherent in the radiological intervention.

The medicolegal review should consider:

- whether sedation was appropriate for the procedure and patient;
- pre-procedure assessment;
- fasting status where applicable;
- allergies and relevant medication;
- airway and cardiorespiratory risk;
- the drugs, doses, routes and times administered;
- supplemental oxygen;
- physiological monitoring;
- the level of consciousness;
- personnel present and their respective roles;
- availability of reversal agents and resuscitation equipment;
- adverse events and interventions;
- recovery observations; and
- criteria for discharge or ward transfer.

The appropriate level of monitoring depends on the patient, medicines used, depth of sedation and procedural setting.

Sedation records should be contemporaneous. Retrospective reconstruction from incomplete observations or medication charts may be unreliable.

## Radiation Exposure

Radiation protection and informed consent are related but distinct.

Under the Ionising Radiation (Medical Exposure) Regulations 2017, medical exposures must be justified and optimised. The employer, referrer, practitioner and operator have separate statutory functions.

For interventional procedures, the relevant record may include:

- fluoroscopy time;
- dose-area product;
- cumulative reference air kerma or equivalent equipment output;
- acquisition runs;
- magnification and collimation;
- relevant dose notifications or alerts;
- technical difficulties that prolonged the procedure;
- measures taken to reduce exposure;
- the operator and equipment used; and
- any post-procedure assessment required because of the exposure.

Fluoroscopy time alone is not a complete measure of patient dose. It should not be interpreted in isolation from the available dose metrics, procedural complexity and equipment factors.

## Radiation Risk and Consent

Radiation risk should be discussed where it is material to the individual decision.

Relevant factors may include:

- the expected exposure;
- whether the procedure may be prolonged or repeated;
- pregnancy or possible pregnancy;
- previous relevant exposure;
- age;
- radiosensitive anatomy;
- the possibility of tissue reactions in a high-dose procedure; and
- whether a non-ionising alternative is reasonable.

Not every procedure requires a detailed numerical explanation of stochastic radiation risk. The information should be proportionate, comprehensible and relevant to the patient's decision.

A technical dose figure without clinical explanation may not assist meaningful consent.

## Optimisation

Dose documentation records what occurred. It does not by itself establish whether the exposure was reasonable.

The review should consider whether exposure was kept as low as reasonably practicable while achieving the intended clinical purpose.

Relevant factors include:

- the complexity and urgency of the procedure;
- operator experience;
- equipment and dose-saving functions;
- collimation;
- pulse and frame rates;
- use of magnification;
- acquisition frequency;
- unnecessary repetition;
- patient habitus;
- procedural difficulty; and
- whether the endpoint justified continued exposure.

A longer or higher-dose procedure is not necessarily negligent. Complex anatomy, haemodynamic instability or difficulty achieving haemostasis may reasonably increase exposure.

Conversely, complexity does not remove the requirement to optimise and document the procedure.

## Unexpectedly High or Unintended Exposure

Where exposure is accidental, unintended or significantly greater or different from that intended, the incident may require investigation under local IR(ME)R procedures.

Depending on the circumstances, the employer may need to:

- preserve the dose and equipment records;
- investigate how the event occurred;
- assess its significance;
- obtain medical physics advice;
- inform relevant duty holders;
- inform the patient or representative where the exposure is clinically significant;
- arrange appropriate clinical follow-up; and
- notify the enforcing authority where the statutory criteria are met.

Not every high-dose procedure is an accidental or unintended exposure. The clinical purpose, complexity and intended conduct of the procedure remain relevant.

## Procedural Complications

Complications may occur despite appropriate consent and competent technique.

The relevant medicolegal questions are:

1. Was the complication a recognised risk?
2. Was the risk material and discussed where required?
3. Were reasonable steps taken to reduce it?
4. Was the procedure performed competently?
5. Was the complication recognised promptly?
6. Was appropriate treatment or escalation provided?
7. Was the event documented accurately?
8. Was the patient informed appropriately?
9. Did any delay in recognition or treatment cause additional harm?

Examples may include:

- haemorrhage;
- vascular injury;
- organ perforation;
- infection;
- contrast reaction;
- non-target embolisation;
- thromboembolic events;
- pneumothorax;
- sedation-related respiratory compromise; and
- radiation-related tissue injury.

The occurrence of a recognised complication is not proof of negligence. Liability depends on the care taken before, during and after the event and on whether any breach caused additional injury.

## Candour

Where care has caused, or may have caused, harm, professional and organisational duties of candour may arise.

The response should be timely, factual and proportionate. It may include:

- explaining what is known;
- acknowledging uncertainty where the investigation is incomplete;
- apologising;
- describing the immediate clinical response;
- outlining further treatment or monitoring;
- explaining that the event will be reviewed;
- documenting the discussion; and
- updating the patient when further information becomes available.

An apology is not, by itself, an admission of legal liability.

Candour should not be confused with speculation. The clinician should not attribute fault before the facts have been established, but should not delay necessary disclosure merely because the final investigation is incomplete.

## Addenda and Corrections

Where a report or procedural record requires correction, the original entry should remain identifiable.

An amendment should ordinarily record:

- what has changed;
- why it has changed;
- who made the amendment;
- the date and time;
- whether management may be affected; and
- who was notified.

A material correction should not rely on passive discovery within the electronic record. If the amendment may alter management, active communication should be considered and documented.

## Medicolegal Review

The expert should separate the following issues:

### Consent

Was the patient given information about material risks and reasonable alternatives in a form they could understand?

### Sedation

Were assessment, medicine administration, monitoring, recovery and any adverse events managed and recorded appropriately?

### Radiation protection

Was the exposure justified, optimised and documented in accordance with the applicable regulatory framework and local procedures?

### Technical performance

Was the procedure carried out with the reasonable skill and care expected of a competent interventional radiologist undertaking that work?

### Complication management

Was the complication recognised, treated and escalated promptly?

### Candour

Was the patient informed openly and appropriately after harm occurred or may have occurred?

### Causation

Would different consent, lower exposure, technically different care or earlier complication management probably have altered the outcome?

## Documents to Obtain

A medicolegal review will commonly require:

- the referral and clinical indication;
- pre-procedure imaging;
- the procedure request and justification record;
- clinic correspondence;
- patient information material;
- the signed consent form;
- the contemporaneous consent note;
- the WHO or local procedural checklist;
- the full procedure record;
- nursing and physiological observation charts;
- sedation and medication records;
- fluoroscopy and acquisition logs;
- complete radiation-dose reports;
- equipment-generated dose notifications;
- post-procedure observations;
- imaging obtained during and after the procedure;
- complication and escalation records;
- report addenda;
- incident reports and investigation findings;
- records of communication with the patient; and
- relevant local policies in force at the time.

The documentation should be considered alongside the imaging and clinical evidence. A missing record may limit reconstruction, but it does not automatically establish that the underlying care was negligent.

## Practical Conclusion

Interventional radiology claims rarely turn on one document.

A defensible analysis reconstructs the complete pathway:

1. indication and alternatives;
2. consent;
3. sedation and monitoring;
4. radiation justification and optimisation;
5. technical performance;
6. recognition and management of complications;
7. communication and candour; and
8. causation.

Keeping these issues separate avoids two common errors: treating every complication as negligent and treating a signed consent form as a complete answer to consent.
