## Introduction

Cauda equina syndrome is a spinal emergency in which delay may lead to permanent bladder, bowel, sexual, sensory or lower-limb dysfunction.

Medicolegal claims often involve several potential points of failure:

- failure to recognise red-flag symptoms;
- delay in requesting MRI;
- inappropriate imaging priority;
- delay in acquiring or reporting MRI;
- failure to identify neural compression;
- inadequate communication of a positive examination;
- delay in spinal referral;
- delay in decompression; and
- disagreement about whether earlier treatment would have improved the outcome.

The radiologist's role is important but defined.

Radiological evidence should establish what the examination showed, whether it was properly reported and what an earlier competent report would probably have changed. Questions about clinical examination, surgical timing and neurological recovery may require separate evidence from emergency medicine, spinal surgery, neurology, urology or rehabilitation specialists.

## Illustrative Scenario

The following anonymised scenario illustrates the analytical method. It is not a report of a published judgment.

A patient attended the emergency department with acute lower-back pain, altered urinary sensation and unilateral lower-limb weakness.

MRI of the lumbar spine was performed on the same day. The original report described a mild L4–L5 disc protrusion and stated that no compressive lesion was identified.

The patient remained under clinical review. Spinal referral and decompression occurred several days later after neurological and bladder symptoms progressed.

Subsequent review of the original MRI identified a large inferiorly migrated disc extrusion at L5–S1. The fragment occupied the spinal canal and compressed the cauda equina.

The medicolegal questions were:

1. whether the original MRI was technically adequate;
2. whether the disc extrusion and neural compression were demonstrated;
3. whether a competent radiologist should have identified and escalated the finding;
4. when competent reporting would probably have led to spinal review and surgery; and
5. whether earlier decompression would probably have produced a better outcome.

## Start With the Clinical Chronology

The imaging cannot be assessed in isolation.

The expert should construct a chronology including:

- onset of back and radicular pain;
- onset and progression of saddle sensory disturbance;
- urinary sensation, retention or incontinence;
- bowel and sexual symptoms;
- lower-limb weakness or sensory change;
- clinical examinations;
- bladder-scan findings;
- MRI request and assigned priority;
- time of image acquisition;
- report verification and availability;
- time and method of communication;
- spinal referral;
- repeat clinical assessment;
- surgery; and
- postoperative neurological function.

The chronology should distinguish symptoms reported by the patient from objective findings recorded by clinicians.

Later recollection may be relevant, but it should not silently replace the contemporaneous record.

## The MRI Request

The expert should review the actual request rather than a later summary of it.

Relevant questions include:

- Was cauda equina syndrome expressly suspected?
- Which red-flag symptoms were included?
- Was neurological deterioration documented?
- Was the request classified as emergency, urgent or routine?
- Was the priority altered after vetting?
- Did the radiology department seek clarification?
- Was the request consistent with the local pathway in force at the time?

The referring clinician is responsible for supplying sufficient clinical information and selecting an appropriate urgency.

A radiologist or vetting clinician should not downgrade a clearly stated suspicion of cauda equina syndrome without sufficient clinical information and an appropriate basis.

## Protocol and Acquisition

Before evaluating interpretation, the expert should determine whether the MRI was technically capable of answering the clinical question.

The review should identify:

- anatomical coverage;
- sagittal and axial sequences;
- whether axial images covered the relevant level;
- slice thickness and gaps;
- image orientation;
- motion or other artefact;
- evidence of previous surgery;
- whether contrast was indicated;
- whether the conus and relevant lumbar canal were included; and
- whether additional imaging was required.

Current pathways may use an abbreviated initial protocol to establish whether cauda equina compression is present. The protocol applicable at the date of the examination should be used when assessing historical care.

A limited protocol is not necessarily inadequate if it reliably answers the urgent clinical question.

Conversely, a report should not exclude compression at a level or region that was not adequately examined.

## Independent Review of the MRI

The expert should review the complete original examination before relying on the wording of the original report or the later diagnosis.

The analysis should address:

- the level of abnormality;
- disc morphology;
- migration or sequestration;
- dimensions of the disc material where measurement is useful;
- degree of canal compromise;
- residual cerebrospinal fluid;
- crowding, displacement or compression of the cauda equina;
- lateral recess and foraminal involvement;
- compression of individual nerve roots;
- conus level and appearance;
- additional spinal pathology; and
- relevant incidental findings.

The significance of compression should be described rather than inferred from measurement alone.

There is no single percentage of canal compromise that independently determines clinical cauda equina syndrome. Imaging findings must be considered alongside the clinical presentation.

## Review in More Than One Plane

A suspected extruded or sequestered disc should be assessed across the available planes.

Sagittal images may show:

- the craniocaudal extent of migrated disc material;
- the level of canal compromise;
- continuity or separation from the parent disc; and
- compression across more than one level.

Axial images may show:

- central or paracentral location;
- occupation of the lateral recess;
- displacement of the thecal sac;
- crowding of nerve roots; and
- asymmetry of compression.

The expert should identify whether the abnormality was demonstrated consistently across the study.

A lesion does not become negligent merely because it can be outlined retrospectively. The opinion should explain its conspicuity on the original images without annotation and with appropriate allowance for hindsight.

## What Should the Report Have Said?

Where the MRI demonstrates significant cauda equina compression, a competent report should clearly state:

- the causative lesion;
- the level;
- the severity and distribution of compression;
- whether the appearances are compatible with the suspected clinical emergency;
- any important limitation; and
- the need for urgent spinal assessment where appropriate.

A suitable conclusion might state:

> Large central and right-paracentral L5–S1 disc extrusion with inferior migration, producing severe spinal canal stenosis and compression of the cauda equina. Urgent spinal surgical assessment is required.

The precise wording will depend on the examination.

The report should not rely on ambiguous expressions such as “clinical correlation advised” where the imaging demonstrates a time-critical compressive lesion.

## Interpretation and Reporting Breach

The legal question is not simply whether the later expert can identify the lesion.

The expert should address:

1. whether the lesion was present on the original examination;
2. whether the examination was adequate;
3. how conspicuous the lesion and neural compression were;
4. whether the original conclusion was reasonably open to a competent radiologist;
5. whether further review or additional imaging was required;
6. whether the significance of the appearance should have been communicated urgently; and
7. whether supporting professional opinion is logically defensible.

If a large compressive disc extrusion is clearly demonstrated across appropriate sagittal and axial sequences, a report stating that no compressive lesion is present may fall below the required standard.

That conclusion should still be reasoned from the actual images and reporting circumstances. It should not be based solely on the later diagnosis or adverse outcome.

## Perceptual and Cognitive Error

The omission may be described as perceptual or cognitive, but the label does not decide negligence.

### Perceptual error

The abnormality was present but not detected.

### Cognitive error

The abnormality was detected but incorrectly characterised or given insufficient clinical significance.

A report describing only a minor protrusion where the images show a large extrusion with severe canal compromise may contain both elements.

The expert should identify what occurred rather than using classification as a substitute for analysis.

## Communication of a Positive Examination

A correct report may still fail to protect the patient if a time-critical result is not communicated effectively.

The review should establish:

- when the report was completed;
- whether it was verified;
- whether an urgent alert was generated;
- who was contacted;
- how contact occurred;
- whether receipt was acknowledged;
- what advice was communicated;
- whether escalation occurred when contact failed; and
- when the responsible spinal team reviewed the result.

For a positive MRI demonstrating cauda equina compression, passive release to an electronic system may be insufficient if urgent action is required.

The appropriate communication route should follow the local pathway and should be documented.

## The Reporting Chain

A cauda equina delay may involve more than one breach.

Potential stages include:

1. **Clinical recognition**  
   Were the symptoms and signs adequately assessed?

2. **MRI referral**  
   Was imaging requested with the appropriate urgency?

3. **Vetting and protocol**  
   Was the request prioritised and the examination selected appropriately?

4. **Acquisition**  
   Was MRI performed within the required timescale and with adequate sequences?

5. **Interpretation**  
   Was neural compression identified and characterised correctly?

6. **Communication**  
   Was a positive result escalated promptly?

7. **Spinal review**  
   Did an appropriate clinician act on the result?

8. **Surgery**  
   Was decompression undertaken within a clinically appropriate period?

The expert should attribute responsibility only to stages within their competence.

## Historical and Current Standards

Standards evolve.

The care should be judged against the guidance and service arrangements applicable at the date of the event, not solely against a pathway published later.

Current UK standards describe suspected cauda equina syndrome as a time-dependent emergency. Acute imaging services should provide continuous access to MRI, with imaging undertaken urgently after request and prompt final reporting.

Those standards are relevant to current service design. They should not be applied retrospectively without considering the date of the alleged breach.

## Causation Is a Separate Question

A negligent MRI report does not establish that every later neurological deficit was caused by the reporting error.

The causation analysis requires a specific counterfactual chronology:

1. When should the MRI have been correctly reported?
2. How and when should the finding have been communicated?
3. When would spinal review probably have occurred?
4. When would surgery probably have taken place?
5. What was the patient's neurological condition at that time?
6. Did function materially deteriorate during the period of avoidable delay?
7. Would earlier decompression probably have produced a better outcome?

The radiologist may assist with the first four questions and with the imaging chronology.

The effect of surgical timing on bladder, bowel, sexual, sensory and motor outcome ordinarily requires spinal surgical and neurological evidence.

## No Single Universal Recovery Window

Cauda equina syndrome is time-dependent, but medicolegal causation should not be reduced to a single universal number of hours.

Outcome may depend on:

- the cause of compression;
- completeness of the syndrome;
- duration of symptoms;
- urinary retention;
- severity of neural compression;
- rate of progression;
- pre-existing neurological disease;
- timing of decompression; and
- the particular function being assessed.

Broad statements such as “surgery within 24 hours always prevents permanent injury” or “the outcome is fixed once retention occurs” are unlikely to be adequate without case-specific support.

The opinion should identify the evidence relevant to the particular patient and avoid certainty beyond the available clinical literature.

## Imaging Chronology and Progression

Where more than one MRI is available, comparison may assist with:

- progression of disc extrusion;
- increasing canal compromise;
- development of nerve-root crowding;
- alternative causes of symptoms;
- postoperative decompression; and
- the relationship between imaging and clinical deterioration.

However, a later MRI cannot always establish precisely what the canal or neural structures looked like at every point in the intervening period.

The expert should distinguish direct imaging evidence from clinical inference.

## Structured Reporting

A consistent approach to emergency lumbar MRI may reduce omission.

The report should consider:

- conus termination and appearance;
- spinal canal;
- principal level of disease;
- disc extrusion or sequestration;
- direction of migration;
- severity of canal compromise;
- cauda equina crowding or compression;
- additional levels of stenosis;
- significant alternative pathology; and
- the conclusion and required urgency.

A structured search pattern may support consistency, but a rigid template cannot replace interpretation.

Templates can also produce error if they encourage the reporter to complete expected fields without responding to the actual examination.

## What the Court Needs From the Radiologist

A useful radiological opinion should answer the following questions directly.

### Examination

- What MRI was requested?
- What protocol was performed?
- Was it technically adequate?
- Were the relevant levels covered?

### Findings

- What did the original images show?
- Was there a disc extrusion or sequestration?
- Was the cauda equina compressed?
- How conspicuous was the abnormality?
- Were there material limitations?

### Standard of care

- What should a reasonably competent radiologist have identified?
- What should the report have said?
- Did the result require urgent communication?
- Did the original report fall outside responsible practice?

### Counterfactual

- When should the correct report have been available?
- How should it have been communicated?
- What radiological action would have followed?
- Which causation questions require another speciality?

The expert should separate fact, assumption and opinion throughout.

## Evidence to Obtain

A delayed-diagnosis claim will commonly require:

- the original MRI DICOM data;
- all sequences and localisers;
- the MRI request;
- the recorded clinical indications;
- vetting and priority records;
- booking and acquisition timestamps;
- the original report and any addenda;
- report verification and release times;
- urgent-alert and communication logs;
- emergency, orthopaedic and spinal notes;
- bladder-scan records;
- referral and transfer records;
- operative notes;
- postoperative assessments;
- earlier and later imaging;
- the local CES pathway in force at the time;
- relevant audit or incident-review records; and
- expert evidence on surgical and neurological causation.

Screenshots may illustrate the opinion but should not replace review of the complete examination.

## Practical Point for Radiologists

When reporting MRI for suspected cauda equina syndrome:

- review the clinical indication;
- confirm that the protocol answers the question;
- assess the complete canal in the acquired field;
- correlate sagittal and axial appearances;
- identify the level and cause of compression;
- describe the severity and neural effect clearly;
- avoid reassurance broader than the examination supports;
- communicate a positive time-critical finding promptly; and
- document the communication.

If the images are incomplete or non-diagnostic, the limitation should be escalated rather than concealed by a definitive negative report.

## Practical Point for Instructing Lawyers

The instruction should distinguish the alleged failures.

Ask the radiology expert to address:

1. protocol adequacy;
2. image findings;
3. conspicuity;
4. the reasonable report;
5. urgency and communication;
6. the probable reporting timeline; and
7. the limits of radiological causation evidence.

Avoid asking the radiologist to determine whether earlier surgery would probably have restored bladder, bowel or neurological function unless that question falls within the expert's current competence.

The letter of instruction should identify the relevant date and ask the expert to apply the professional standard in force at that time.

## Relationship With Jarman

*Jarman v Brighton and Sussex University Hospitals NHS Trust* [2021] EWHC 323 (QB) illustrates the distinction between clinical triage and radiological reporting.

In *Jarman*, the court found that the examining orthopaedic registrar had not been negligent in deciding that emergency MRI was not required on the presentation then recorded. The claimant also failed to prove that the later interval caused additional neurological damage.

A claim involving a same-day MRI that clearly demonstrates compression but is incorrectly reported raises a different breach question.

The distinction is between:

- whether MRI should have been requested urgently;
- whether the MRI was adequately performed;
- whether it was correctly interpreted;
- whether the result was communicated; and
- whether any delay caused additional injury.

## Conclusion

The radiologist's contribution to a cauda equina claim should be precise and limited to radiological expertise.

The central questions are:

- Was the MRI appropriate and adequate?
- What did it show?
- Should the compression have been identified?
- What should the report have said?
- Was urgent communication required?
- When would competent reporting probably have led to spinal review?
- Which outcome questions require evidence from another specialty?

A strong opinion does not assume that every delay caused the final disability. It identifies the radiological breach, constructs the likely reporting counterfactual and leaves neurological and surgical causation to appropriately qualified experts.
