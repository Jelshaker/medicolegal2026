## Introduction

Gastrointestinal abnormalities can be difficult to identify on CT. Early disease may produce only subtle changes in bowel-wall thickness or enhancement, adjacent mesenteric fat, vascular patency or luminal calibre.

These appearances are also influenced by the examination performed. Contrast administration, acquisition timing, anatomical coverage, slice thickness, reconstruction and motion can all affect conspicuity.

A later reviewer may identify an abnormality once the diagnosis is known. That retrospective observation is relevant, but it does not by itself establish negligence. The medicolegal question is whether the abnormality should reasonably have been identified and acted upon on the original examination, using the information and imaging available at the time.

## Common Areas of Difficulty

### Bowel-wall abnormality

Focal wall thickening may reflect tumour, inflammation, infection, oedema, haemorrhage or ischaemia. Its significance depends on distribution, symmetry, enhancement, luminal distension and associated mesenteric findings.

An apparently abnormal segment may be difficult to assess where the bowel is collapsed or poorly distended. Conversely, genuine mural disease should not be dismissed solely as under-distension without considering the complete pattern.

### Mesenteric abnormality

Mesenteric oedema, fat stranding, venous congestion, small-volume fluid and subtle vascular abnormalities may be important but non-specific.

The significance of an individual sign depends on the wider examination. A small amount of mesenteric stranding may be incidental, whereas the same finding associated with abnormal bowel enhancement, vessel occlusion or a closed-loop configuration may require urgent escalation.

### Gastrointestinal malignancy

Small primary tumours and early peritoneal or metastatic disease may be inconspicuous on a non-targeted examination. Relevant limitations may include inadequate luminal distension, a non-dedicated protocol, respiratory movement, limited contrast enhancement or the absence of appropriate prior imaging.

The fact that a lesion is visible retrospectively does not resolve whether it was sufficiently conspicuous to require detection at the time.

### Mesenteric ischaemia

Acute mesenteric ischaemia is a time-critical diagnosis with several possible mechanisms, including arterial occlusion, venous thrombosis and non-occlusive hypoperfusion.

Where acute mesenteric ischaemia is clinically suspected, prompt CT angiography is generally the appropriate examination. The study should permit assessment of the mesenteric vessels, bowel enhancement and secondary abdominal findings.

A routine portal-venous CT may demonstrate bowel ischaemia and major vascular pathology, but it may not provide the same arterial assessment as a dedicated CT angiographic examination. The relevance of this limitation depends on the clinical question known when the examination was requested and protocoled.

## The Four Stages of Review

A defensible expert analysis should distinguish four stages.

### 1. Referral

The first question is what clinical information was supplied.

Relevant matters may include:

- the site and character of pain;
- duration and progression of symptoms;
- operative history;
- cardiovascular and thromboembolic risk;
- biochemical abnormalities;
- suspected obstruction or perforation; and
- whether mesenteric ischaemia was specifically considered.

The absence of an explicit diagnosis on the request does not remove the reporter's responsibility to assess the examination. It may, however, affect whether a specialist protocol should reasonably have been selected.

### 2. Protocol

The next question is whether the selected examination was appropriate for the clinical presentation.

The expert should consider:

- whether intravenous contrast was used;
- the timing of contrast acquisition;
- whether an arterial phase was indicated;
- anatomical coverage;
- slice thickness and reconstructions;
- oral contrast where relevant;
- comparison imaging; and
- any documented reason for limiting the examination.

Responsibility for protocol selection may rest with more than one person. Depending on local practice, the decision may involve the referrer, radiographer, protocoling radiologist and provider's written procedures.

### 3. Acquisition

An appropriate protocol may still be inadequately performed.

The review should address:

- contrast delivery and timing;
- motion or other artefact;
- incomplete anatomical coverage;
- reconstruction quality;
- technical failure;
- whether the limitations were recognised; and
- whether repeat or supplementary imaging was required.

If the study was not diagnostic for the clinical question, that limitation should ordinarily be identified rather than allowing a falsely reassuring conclusion.

### 4. Interpretation and communication

Only after the first three stages have been considered should the adequacy of the report be assessed.

The relevant questions include:

- what the study objectively demonstrated;
- whether the abnormality was sufficiently conspicuous;
- whether the reporter considered an appropriate differential;
- whether uncertainty was communicated;
- whether further imaging or clinical review was advised;
- whether a time-critical finding was escalated; and
- whether the report directly addressed the clinical question.

## Demonstrability and Hindsight

The expert should review the original source images where available, rather than relying on screenshots, selected images or later annotations.

The analysis should take account of:

- the full examination;
- the original technical quality;
- clinical information available at the time;
- relevant prior imaging;
- accepted practice at the date of reporting; and
- the effect of knowing the eventual diagnosis.

A finding may be visible after it has been pointed out without having been sufficiently conspicuous to make the original report negligent. Equally, describing an abnormality as “subtle” does not answer the legal question if its location, extent and associated features should reasonably have prompted recognition or further investigation.

The opinion should explain why the finding did or did not fall outside the range of responsible interpretation.

## Protocol Failure or Reporting Failure?

The distinction is important.

If the required information was absent because an inappropriate examination was selected, the principal issue may concern referral or protocol.

If the appropriate images were acquired but the abnormality was not identified or was incorrectly characterised, the principal issue may concern interpretation.

If the report identified the relevant abnormality but it was not conveyed or acted upon with appropriate urgency, the issue may concern communication or the subsequent clinical pathway.

More than one failure may be present. The expert should not attribute the entire pathway to the reporting radiologist without analysing the responsibility attached to each stage.

## Causation

Even where breach is established, the claimant must ordinarily prove that a competent report would probably have produced a materially different outcome.

The causation analysis should identify:

1. what the competent report should have said;
2. when that report would have been available;
3. what clinical action would probably have followed;
4. what treatment would then have been possible; and
5. whether that treatment would probably have avoided or reduced the injury.

Radiological evidence may establish when an abnormality was present and what earlier imaging should have prompted. Questions concerning surgery, treatment selection, bowel viability, survival or long-term prognosis may require evidence from other specialties.

## Material to Obtain

A gastrointestinal delayed-diagnosis claim should ordinarily be reviewed using:

- the original DICOM examinations;
- all relevant series and reconstructions;
- the imaging requests and clinical details;
- contemporaneous reports and addenda;
- protocol and contrast information;
- earlier and later comparative imaging;
- laboratory and operative records where relevant;
- records of urgent communication;
- local protocol documents in force at the time; and
- evidence from the relevant treating specialties where causation extends beyond radiology.

## Practical Conclusion

Missed gastrointestinal findings should not be analysed as a simple comparison between the original report and the eventual diagnosis.

The proper approach is to determine:

- what clinical question was presented;
- whether the correct examination was selected;
- whether it was adequately acquired;
- what the original images demonstrated;
- whether the interpretation and communication were reasonable; and
- what earlier recognition would probably have changed.

This separates a genuine reporting failure from a limitation of referral, technique or disease conspicuity, while preserving the distinct question of causation.
