Gastrointestinal abnormalities are frequently difficult to detect on CT because early disease often produces only subtle alterations in bowel-wall thickness, enhancement, mesenteric fat, vascular patency, or luminal calibre. Conspicuity is further influenced by technical variables including contrast administration, acquisition timing, anatomical coverage, slice thickness, reconstruction quality, and motion artefact. A later reviewer may identify an abnormality once the diagnosis is known, but retrospective observation alone does not establish negligence. The medicolegal standard requires determining whether the abnormality should reasonably have been identified and acted upon using only the imaging and clinical information available at the time of the original examination.

Bowel-wall thickening can result from malignancy, inflammation, infection, oedema, or ischaemia, but its clinical significance depends on distribution, symmetry, enhancement pattern, luminal distension, and associated mesenteric findings. Collapsed bowel may mask disease, yet genuine mural pathology should not be dismissed solely as under-distension without evaluating the complete imaging pattern. Mesenteric abnormalities such as oedema, fat stranding, venous congestion, or small-volume fluid are frequently non-specific; their importance depends entirely on contextual findings like abnormal bowel enhancement, vessel occlusion, or closed-loop configurations. Small primary tumours and early peritoneal or metastatic disease may also be inconspicuous on non-dedicated examinations due to inadequate luminal distension, respiratory motion, limited contrast enhancement, or the absence of comparative imaging. In suspected acute mesenteric ischaemia, imaging must reliably assess both vascular patency and bowel viability. A routine portal-venous CT may detect major ischaemia and large vessel pathology but typically lacks the arterial detail of a dedicated CT angiogram, making the appropriateness of the protocol contingent on the clinical question known at the time of request.

A defensible medicolegal review systematically evaluates four sequential stages before attributing responsibility. First, the referral must be examined for relevant clinical details, including symptom characteristics, duration, progression, operative history, cardiovascular risk factors, biochemical abnormalities, and whether mesenteric ischaemia was specifically suspected. The absence of a definitive diagnosis on the request does not remove the reporting radiologist's duty but may inform whether a specialist protocol was warranted. Second, the protocol must be assessed for technical appropriateness, including contrast use, phase timing, anatomical coverage, slice thickness, reconstructions, oral contrast, and comparison imaging. Responsibility for protocol selection is typically shared among the referrer, radiographer, and radiologist according to local practice. Third, acquisition quality must be verified; technical limitations such as poor contrast timing, motion artefact, incomplete coverage, or reconstruction errors must be identified rather than overlooked. If the study was non-diagnostic for the clinical question, this limitation should be explicitly noted rather than masked by a falsely reassuring report. Only after these technical factors are accounted for should interpretation and communication be evaluated. This includes assessing whether the abnormality was sufficiently conspicuous, whether appropriate differentials were considered, whether uncertainty was clearly communicated, whether urgent findings were escalated, and whether the report directly addressed the presenting clinical question.

Retrospective analysis must rely on original DICOM source data rather than screenshots or later annotations. The expert must account for the full examination, original technical quality, contemporaneous clinical information, relevant prior imaging, and accepted practice at the date of reporting, while consciously avoiding hindsight bias. A finding visible only after being pointed out does not establish negligence, nor does labelling a finding “subtle” automatically indicate a breach if its characteristics warranted recognition or further investigation. The opinion must clearly explain why the original interpretation did or did not fall outside the range of responsible clinical practice. Responsibility for any failure must be traced to its source: inappropriate clinical questioning or protocol selection indicates a referral or planning failure, whereas adequately acquired images with missed or mischaracterised findings point to an interpretive error. If a finding was correctly identified but inadequately communicated or acted upon, the breakdown lies in clinical communication or management. Multiple failures may coexist, and responsibility should not be automatically assigned to the reporting radiologist without evaluating each stage.

Even when a reporting error is established, the claimant must prove that a competent interpretation would likely have altered the outcome. The causation analysis requires tracing a clear chain: what the correct report should have stated, when it would have become available, what clinical actions would probably have followed, what treatments would then have been possible, and whether those interventions would likely have avoided or reduced harm. Radiological evidence can establish when an abnormality was present and what earlier imaging should have prompted, but questions regarding surgical intervention, treatment selection, bowel viability, survival, or long-term prognosis require input from the treating specialties. An effective review therefore requires a comprehensive dataset including original DICOM studies, all reconstructions, clinical requests, contemporaneous reports, protocol documentation, comparative imaging, laboratory and operative records, evidence of urgent communications, and local protocols in force at the time. Rather than comparing a missed finding to a later diagnosis, the analysis must follow a structured framework that separates genuine reporting errors from technical limitations or disease characteristics, while preserving the distinct legal requirements for breach and causation.
