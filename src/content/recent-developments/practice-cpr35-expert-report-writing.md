## Introduction

A radiological expert report is not a clinical report with legal terminology added.

Its purpose is to assist the court on defined matters within the expert's competence. It should explain:

- what material was reviewed;
- what the imaging objectively demonstrates;
- which facts are assumed;
- what professional opinion follows;
- why that opinion is held;
- whether a reasonable range of opinion exists;
- what limitations affect the conclusion; and
- which questions require evidence from another discipline.

The report belongs to the court process rather than to the case theory of the instructing party.

A report is not strengthened by appearing certain. It is strengthened by being independent, transparent, reasoned and confined to the available evidence.

## The Governing Framework

Expert evidence in civil proceedings in England and Wales is principally governed by:

- CPR Part 35;
- Practice Direction 35;
- the Guidance for the Instruction of Experts in Civil Claims 2014;
- relevant court orders;
- applicable pre-action protocols;
- the expert's professional obligations; and
- any additional procedural requirements applying to the particular proceedings.

The expert should work from the current versions in force when the report is prepared.

### Evidence must be reasonably required

Expert evidence is restricted to that which is reasonably required to resolve the proceedings.

This has practical consequences. A radiologist should not duplicate evidence that falls properly within another specialty or provide extensive commentary on matters that do not assist resolution of the pleaded issues.

### Permission is required

A party may not call an expert or rely on an expert report without the court's permission.

Permission may be limited to:

- a named expert;
- a particular discipline;
- specified issues;
- a particular report;
- an agreed budget; or
- a single joint expert.

The report should remain within the field and issues for which permission has been given.

### Overriding duty

The expert's duty is to help the court on matters within their expertise.

That duty overrides any obligation to:

- the person giving instructions;
- the party who selected the expert;
- the organisation employing the expert; or
- the person paying the expert's fees.

The same duty applies whether the expert is instructed by a claimant, defendant, several parties jointly or as a single joint expert.

## Independence

Expert evidence should be the independent product of the expert, uninfluenced by the pressures of litigation.

The expert should not:

- assume the role of an advocate;
- omit evidence because it is inconvenient to the instructing party;
- adopt disputed facts without identifying them as assumptions;
- tailor the opinion to the desired outcome;
- stray outside current expertise;
- express certainty unsupported by the evidence; or
- preserve an opinion after the evidential basis has changed.

The expert should consider all material facts, including those that may detract from the opinion.

A balanced report is not weakened by acknowledging contrary material. Failure to address it is more likely to undermine the evidence.

## The Single Joint Expert

A single joint expert is instructed to prepare evidence for the court on behalf of two or more parties.

The expert is not:

- the claimant's expert;
- the defendant's expert;
- a mediator;
- an advocate;
- an arbitrator; or
- the final decision-maker.

The court remains responsible for deciding the disputed issues.

### Joint instructions

The parties should seek to agree the letter of instruction and the questions to be addressed.

Where instructions are disputed or incomplete, the expert should not privately resolve the dispute with one party. The expert may need to:

- request clarification from all instructing parties;
- identify the ambiguity in the report;
- proceed on clearly stated alternative assumptions; or
- seek directions through the proper procedural route.

### Communications

Substantive communications with a single joint expert should ordinarily be transparent to all instructing parties.

The expert should avoid private communication with one party about:

- the merits;
- disputed evidence;
- additional questions;
- assumptions;
- provisional conclusions; or
- proposed changes to the report.

Administrative communication may be unavoidable, but it should not create an appearance of partiality.

### Further material

If one party supplies additional material, the expert should establish whether it has also been provided to the other parties and whether it falls within the instruction.

The expert should keep an accurate record of the material received and the date of receipt.

## Expert Witness and Expert Adviser

The distinction is important.

An expert adviser may provide confidential preliminary advice to a party. If the adviser is not intended to give evidence, Part 35 may not yet govern that advisory work in the same way.

An expert witness prepares or gives evidence for the proceedings and owes the overriding duty to the court.

An expert initially instructed only to advise may later become the expert witness. This should be considered carefully because:

- earlier advice may have been given on incomplete material;
- the expert's role changes;
- prior communications may become relevant to independence;
- the expert must reassess the case objectively; and
- the final report must comply fully with Part 35 and Practice Direction 35.

The report should not conceal a material change from an earlier opinion.

## Define the Question Before Reviewing the Images

A high-quality opinion begins with a clear issue.

Examples include:

- whether a fracture was visible on the original radiograph;
- whether a CT demonstrated a gastrointestinal malignancy;
- whether an MRI showed cauda equina compression;
- whether an incidental abnormality required reporting;
- whether the imaging protocol was adequate;
- whether a report required urgent escalation; or
- what competent reporting would probably have changed.

The expert should distinguish the radiological question from broader questions such as:

- whether surgery was indicated;
- whether treatment would have succeeded;
- life expectancy;
- future care;
- legal causation;
- contributory negligence; or
- the ultimate issue of liability.

Some overlap may be unavoidable, but disciplinary boundaries should be stated clearly.

## The Material Reviewed

The report should identify the material relied upon.

For a radiological opinion this may include:

- the letter of instruction;
- statements of case;
- witness statements;
- clinical records;
- imaging requests;
- original DICOM examinations;
- contemporaneous reports and addenda;
- previous and subsequent imaging;
- operative records;
- pathology;
- multidisciplinary records;
- local policies;
- professional guidance;
- literature; and
- other expert reports.

The list should distinguish:

- material received but not relied upon;
- material reviewed after the initial opinion;
- missing material;
- illegible or incomplete records; and
- images supplied only as screenshots or compressed exports.

The expert should not imply review of source imaging where only selected images were provided.

## Confirm Identity and Chronology

Before interpretation, the expert should verify, so far as the available material permits:

- patient or case identifier;
- examination date;
- modality;
- body region;
- accession or study identity;
- series completeness;
- report date;
- relevant prior and subsequent studies; and
- the chronology of clinical events.

Where material has been anonymised, the expert should confirm that the imaging and records can still be linked reliably.

Anonymisation should not make the evidential chronology uncertain.

## Findings, Facts and Opinions

The distinction between fact and opinion is essential, but radiological evidence does not divide into two perfectly separate columns.

### Facts within the expert's own knowledge

These may include:

- the files received;
- the examination reviewed;
- the sequences or series present;
- measurements personally made;
- findings personally observed; and
- limitations apparent from the images.

### Assumed or documentary facts

These may include:

- symptoms;
- examination findings;
- treatment dates;
- operative events;
- what the original reporter knew;
- the identity of the person who performed a procedure; and
- the clinical consequences said to have followed.

These facts derive from records or instructions and should be identified accordingly.

### Opinion

Opinion includes conclusions such as:

- the examination was technically adequate;
- an abnormality should have been identified;
- the original interpretation fell outside responsible practice;
- further imaging was required;
- an urgent alert should have been issued; or
- the imaging supports a particular clinical counterfactual.

The expert should explain the reasoning linking the underlying facts and images to the opinion.

### Conclusions on the images

Statements such as “there is a fracture” or “the MRI demonstrates neural compression” may be expert interpretations of objective imaging evidence.

They should not be treated as immune from analysis merely by labelling them factual. Where interpretation is disputed, the report should explain the imaging features supporting the conclusion.

## Review the Original Imaging

The original source examination should be reviewed where reasonably available.

The expert should record:

- the format supplied;
- whether the study appears complete;
- modality and protocol;
- relevant series;
- image quality;
- reconstructions;
- comparison imaging;
- software or workstation used where material;
- measurements undertaken; and
- technical limitations.

There is rarely a need to describe every ordinary feature of the viewing environment.

Technical detail should be included where it helps the court understand:

- what was available;
- why a finding was or was not demonstrable;
- whether post-processing altered conspicuity;
- whether the material supplied was incomplete; or
- why the opinion is qualified.

## Re-derivation and Hindsight

A retrospective expert knows the eventual diagnosis and often has access to later imaging, pathology and operative findings.

That knowledge creates hindsight risk.

A disciplined review may include:

1. reviewing the original examination before later imaging where practicable;
2. identifying the clinical information available at the relevant time;
3. recording the appearance without annotation;
4. separating direct signs from findings recognised only after the diagnosis was known;
5. comparing later evidence only after the initial review; and
6. explaining how later information affects confidence.

The expert should not claim to reproduce the original reporter's exact mental process or workstation state unless the evidence supports that claim.

## Structure of the Report

There is no single compulsory stylistic template for every radiological report. The report must, however, include the content required by the rules, Practice Direction and applicable court order.

A practical structure is set out below.

## 1. Heading and Court Details

Include the information required for the proceedings, such as:

- the court;
- claim number;
- parties;
- report title;
- expert's name and discipline;
- instructing party or joint instruction;
- report date; and
- status of the report.

The report should be addressed to the court rather than to the instructing solicitor.

## 2. Expert's Qualifications and Experience

Set out the qualifications and experience relevant to the particular issues.

This may include:

- current appointment;
- specialist registration;
- subspecialty practice;
- relevant procedural experience;
- reporting volume where material;
- teaching or research directly relevant to the issue;
- expert-witness training; and
- previous expert experience where appropriate.

A complete curriculum vitae may be appended.

The section should demonstrate competence without becoming promotional.

## 3. Declaration of Duty

The report should contain the required declaration that the expert understands and has complied with the duty to the court and is aware of the applicable procedural requirements.

The wording should follow the current prescribed form rather than a personally drafted approximation.

## 4. Instructions and Questions

State:

- who instructed the expert;
- whether the instruction is party-appointed or joint;
- the date of instruction;
- the questions asked;
- any court order defining the issues;
- any agreed assumptions; and
- any disputed instructions.

The expert should answer the questions directly but is not confined to an erroneous premise.

Where a question cannot be answered as framed, the report should explain why and identify what can properly be answered.

## 5. Material Considered

Provide a structured list of the records, imaging and literature considered.

For imaging, identify:

- examination date;
- modality;
- anatomical region;
- relevant series; and
- whether source DICOM, another full-fidelity format or selected images were supplied.

## 6. Factual Background

Summarise only the facts necessary to understand the opinion.

Distinguish:

- agreed facts;
- disputed facts;
- facts stated in the instructions;
- facts derived from records; and
- facts within the expert's own knowledge.

Lengthy repetition of the entire clinical record can obscure the issues and should usually be avoided.

## 7. Imaging Chronology

A chronological table or structured narrative may be useful where several examinations are involved.

For each study, record:

- date;
- indication;
- technique;
- principal findings;
- contemporaneous report; and
- relevance to the questions instructed.

The chronology should not blend the expert's retrospective interpretation with the original report without identifying the difference.

## 8. Independent Imaging Findings

Set out the expert's review of the images before addressing breach.

The findings should be:

- clear;
- anatomically precise;
- appropriately measured;
- linked to the relevant images;
- proportionate to the issues; and
- understandable to a non-radiologist.

Annotated figures may assist where they are:

- representative;
- accurately labelled;
- traceable to the source examination;
- explained in the text; and
- not used as a substitute for reviewing the complete study.

## 9. Professional Standard

Identify the standard against which the original care is assessed.

This may require discussion of:

- the expertise reasonably expected of the original reporter;
- the clinical setting;
- the examination type;
- the relevant date;
- professional guidance then in force;
- local practice where properly evidenced;
- the reasonable range of interpretation; and
- the effect of technical limitations.

The expert should not define the legal test. The expert provides professional opinion to assist the court in applying it.

## 10. Breach of Duty

Address each alleged breach separately.

For example:

- inadequate protocol;
- failure to identify a finding;
- incorrect characterisation;
- failure to recommend further imaging;
- failure to communicate an urgent result; or
- inadequate addendum or correction.

For each issue, explain:

1. what competent care required;
2. what occurred;
3. why the difference was or was not material;
4. whether a reasonable range of professional opinion exists; and
5. the conclusion.

Avoid conclusions such as “the defendant was negligent.” A preferable formulation is:

> In my opinion, the report fell below the standard reasonably expected of a competent consultant radiologist undertaking this work at the relevant time.

The ultimate legal determination belongs to the court.

## 11. Causation Within Radiological Expertise

The expert should identify what competent radiological care would probably have changed.

This may include:

- the wording of the competent report;
- urgency;
- further imaging;
- communication;
- referral;
- probable date of diagnosis; and
- imaging stage at the relevant time.

The radiologist should not offer unsupported opinions on:

- surgical outcome;
- oncological treatment;
- survival;
- neurological recovery;
- psychiatric injury;
- care needs; or
- legal causation outside radiological competence.

The report should expressly identify which causal questions require another specialty.

## 12. Range of Opinion

Where a reasonable range of professional opinion exists, the report must summarise that range and give reasons for the expert's own view.

A useful analysis may state:

- the competing interpretations;
- the evidence supporting each;
- the points on which responsible experts may differ;
- why the expert prefers one interpretation; and
- the degree of confidence in the conclusion.

The existence of disagreement does not automatically mean that all views are reasonable.

Equally, the expert should not present a genuinely disputed and defensible interpretation as though only one competent view were possible.

## 13. Assumptions

Material assumptions should be stated expressly.

Examples include:

- that the supplied DICOM is complete;
- that the request information reproduced in the records was available to the reporter;
- that no relevant prior imaging was accessible;
- that the timestamps are accurate;
- that a stated clinical event occurred;
- or that the court accepts one of two disputed factual accounts.

Where the opinion differs under alternative assumptions, explain both outcomes.

## 14. Limitations

Limitations may include:

- missing source images;
- incomplete clinical records;
- absence of prior imaging;
- poor image quality;
- uncertainty about what information was available;
- inability to reproduce the original display environment;
- inability to determine progression between examinations;
- conflict between records; or
- a question outside the expert's discipline.

A limitation should identify its effect.

For example:

> The original axial source images have not been provided. I can assess the saved reformats, but I cannot determine whether the disputed finding was visible on the complete acquisition. My opinion on breach is therefore provisional.

Generic disclaimers add little.

## 15. Material That Could Change the Opinion

The report should identify missing evidence that could materially alter the conclusion.

This may include:

- a prior examination;
- complete DICOM data;
- the original imaging request;
- an unprovided report addendum;
- operative findings;
- histopathology;
- communication logs;
- a disputed witness account; or
- evidence from another specialty.

There is no prescribed requirement for a specially titled “what would change my opinion” paragraph in every report.

It is nevertheless good practice to make the evidential dependencies clear, particularly where the opinion is provisional or based on disputed assumptions.

## 16. Answers to the Questions

Answer each instructed question directly.

Responses should avoid unnecessary repetition but should cross-refer to the supporting reasoning.

Useful formulations include:

- “Yes, for the reasons set out at paragraphs…”
- “No. The examination did not demonstrate…”
- “I cannot reach a concluded opinion because…”
- “My opinion depends on whether the court finds that…”
- “This question falls outside my radiological expertise.”

## 17. Summary of Conclusions

Provide a concise summary of the principal conclusions.

It should accurately reflect the detailed reasoning and avoid introducing new opinion.

A summary is particularly useful where the report addresses several examinations, allegations or counterfactual stages.

## 18. Declarations and Statement of Truth

The report must contain the declarations and statement of truth required by the current procedural rules and Practice Direction.

The expert should use the prescribed wording current at the date of signature.

The statement of truth is not a formality. The expert should ensure that:

- the final report has been reviewed in full;
- amendments are incorporated correctly;
- annexes and figures are accurate;
- opinions remain current;
- qualifications are clear; and
- no material contrary evidence has been omitted.

## Literature and Professional Guidance

The report should identify literature or other material relied upon.

Sources should be:

- relevant to the issue;
- applicable to the date in question;
- represented accurately;
- placed in clinical context; and
- distinguished from mandatory legal requirements.

Professional guidance may assist the court in understanding accepted practice, but departure from guidance does not automatically establish negligence.

Likewise, compliance with guidance does not conclusively determine that care was reasonable.

The expert should explain the relevance and status of each source.

## Use of Measurements

Radiological measurements may assist the court, but false precision should be avoided.

The report should state where material:

- the image or sequence used;
- the plane of measurement;
- the anatomical landmarks;
- whether the measurement was reproduced;
- likely measurement variability;
- whether magnification or calibration affects the result; and
- whether the threshold has genuine clinical significance.

A different measurement by another expert is not necessarily evidence that either expert is unreliable. The difference may arise from technique, plane selection or biological ambiguity.

## Use of Images

Figures can improve understanding where they are selected carefully.

Each figure should include:

- examination date;
- modality and sequence or series;
- image number where available;
- orientation;
- concise annotation;
- a caption explaining the relevant feature; and
- confirmation that the figure derives from the reviewed source examination.

Annotations should not imply that a finding was equally conspicuous without them.

The report should distinguish illustrative figures from the complete examination on which the opinion is based.

## Avoiding Advocacy

Advocacy often appears through language rather than overt partisanship.

Expressions to avoid include:

- “clearly negligent”;
- “indefensible” without analysis;
- “obviously visible”;
- “the claimant plainly proves”;
- “the defendant's excuse”;
- “no competent radiologist could disagree” unless genuinely supportable; and
- commentary on witness credibility outside the expert's proper role.

Prefer language such as:

- “In my opinion…”;
- “The images demonstrate…”;
- “A reasonable range of opinion exists…”;
- “I consider that the report fell below…”;
- “I cannot determine…”;
- “If the court accepts X, my opinion is…”; and
- “This matter requires evidence from…”

Neutral language does not require a neutral conclusion. Where the evidence supports a firm opinion, it should be stated clearly.

## Dealing With Uncertainty

An expert may reach:

- a definite opinion;
- a qualified opinion;
- a provisional opinion;
- an opinion dependent on a factual finding; or
- no concluded opinion.

The report should explain which applies and why.

It is preferable to identify uncertainty than to disguise it through vague wording.

Where probabilities are used, explain their basis. Numerical percentages should not be invented where the evidence supports only a qualitative conclusion.

## Changes of Opinion

If the expert's opinion changes materially after the report is produced, the change should be communicated without delay to those instructing the expert and, where appropriate, to the court.

The expert should explain:

- what changed;
- which new evidence prompted the change;
- which conclusions are affected;
- whether the earlier report should be withdrawn or supplemented; and
- whether the change affects a joint statement or forthcoming hearing.

Changing an opinion for a proper evidential reason is consistent with the expert's duty.

Defending an obsolete opinion because it has already been disclosed is not.

## Questions Under CPR 35.6

A party may put written questions to an expert for clarification of the report within the procedural framework.

Responses become part of the expert evidence.

The expert should:

- answer the question asked;
- avoid advocacy;
- identify any new assumption;
- correct genuine errors;
- explain where the question requires material outside the original instruction;
- avoid substantive new work without clarification of authority and fees; and
- preserve consistency with the duty to the court.

The rules governing timing and scope should be checked in the current CPR and any court order.

## Discussions Between Experts

The purpose of an expert discussion is not to negotiate a compromise.

The experts should identify:

- issues agreed;
- issues not agreed;
- reasons for disagreement;
- any further material required; and
- whether the disagreement depends on factual assumptions.

The joint statement should accurately record the experts' own opinions.

Lawyers may assist with the agenda and procedural arrangements but should not dictate the substance of the expert agreement.

An expert should not agree a proposition merely to reduce the issues if it does not represent their genuine opinion.

## Oral Evidence

A report that is clear, transparent and properly reasoned is easier to defend in oral evidence.

Preparation should include:

- rereading the report;
- reviewing the material relied upon;
- identifying any corrections;
- revisiting the opposing opinion;
- understanding the areas of agreement and disagreement;
- being able to locate the relevant images;
- explaining the reasoning in plain language; and
- remaining willing to reconsider a proposition where appropriate.

The expert should answer the question asked, identify any necessary qualification and avoid argument with counsel.

Cross-examination tests the reasoning. It should not alter the expert's duty.

## Common Weaknesses in Radiological Reports

### Starting With the Desired Conclusion

The expert adopts the instructing party's theory and searches the images for support.

### Treating Retrospective Visibility as Breach

The finding is visible after annotation or later diagnosis, but prospective conspicuity is not analysed.

### Ignoring the Clinical Context

The expert relies on information that was not available to the original reporter.

### Failing to Review Source Imaging

The opinion is based on reports, screenshots or selected images.

### Giving Opinions Outside Radiology

The report decides surgical timing, prognosis or treatment outcome without appropriate expertise.

### Avoiding the Reasonable Range

The expert presents one interpretation as mandatory without addressing a responsible alternative.

### Quoting Guidance Without Analysis

Professional standards are reproduced but their relevance, date and status are not explained.

### Mixing Breach and Causation

The report assumes that because an abnormality should have been reported, every later consequence was caused by the omission.

### Failing to Address Contrary Evidence

The report does not engage with images, records or opinions that weaken the conclusion.

### Excessive Certainty

The wording is stronger than the imaging or clinical evidence permits.

## A Practical Radiological Report Structure

A concise working structure may be:

1. Court and claim details  
2. Expert's identity, qualifications and relevant experience  
3. Declaration of duty  
4. Instructions and issues  
5. Material considered  
6. Relevant factual background  
7. Imaging chronology  
8. Independent imaging findings  
9. Professional standard  
10. Breach analysis  
11. Radiological causation and counterfactual  
12. Reasonable range of opinion  
13. Assumptions and limitations  
14. Material capable of changing the opinion  
15. Answers to questions  
16. Summary of conclusions  
17. References  
18. Required declarations and statement of truth  
19. Appendices and figures  

This is a practical structure, not a substitute for the current procedural requirements or a court order.

## Practical Checklist Before Signature

Before signing, the expert should ask:

- Have I addressed only matters within my competence?
- Have I complied with the court's permission and directions?
- Have I reviewed the best available imaging?
- Have I identified the material relied upon?
- Have I distinguished my observations, assumptions and opinions?
- Have I addressed evidence that detracts from my view?
- Have I explained the reasonable range of opinion?
- Have I given reasons for my conclusions?
- Have I separated breach from causation?
- Have I identified questions requiring another specialty?
- Have I stated material limitations?
- Have I identified anything that could change my opinion?
- Have I used the current required declarations and statement of truth?
- Is the report addressed to and written for the court?
- Would I express the same opinion if instructed by the opposing party?

If the answer to the final question is no, the report should not be signed.

## Conclusion

A strong Part 35 radiological report is not defined by length or certainty.

It is defined by:

- relevant expertise;
- independence;
- accurate review of the source imaging;
- clear identification of facts and assumptions;
- reasoned professional opinion;
- consideration of contrary material;
- acknowledgment of the reasonable range;
- careful separation of breach and causation;
- transparency about limitations; and
- compliance with the procedural requirements.

The single joint expert is not a diluted or neutralised party expert. The role carries the same overriding duty to help the court, with an additional need for transparent communication with all instructing parties.

A report withstands scrutiny when the reasoning is visible, the limits are candid and the conclusion follows from the evidence rather than from the identity of the party who instructed the expert.
