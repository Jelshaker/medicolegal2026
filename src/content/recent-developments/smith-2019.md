## **R (Smith) v Castle Point Borough Council**

**[2019] EWHC 2019 (Admin)**  
**High Court, Queen's Bench Division, Planning Court**  
**Mr C M G Ockelton, Vice President of the Upper Tribunal**  
**Judgment delivered: 31 July 2019**

<br><br>

### Background

The claimant challenged a local authority's decision to grant planning permission, partly retrospectively, for a 5-metre boundary wall surrounding a scrapyard operated by the interested party. The claimant, who owned adjoining land, argued that the authority had failed properly to consider contamination issues, the site's planning history, the potential for intensified use of the scrapyard, and the impact of the wall on the openness and appearance of the Green Belt.

### Decision

The High Court dismissed the judicial review.

### Key Legal Principles

#### 1. Contaminated land and planning permission

The court held that planning policies and guidance concerning contaminated land did not require contamination assessments simply because development was proposed on land already known or suspected to be contaminated. The relevant guidance was principally directed at developments involving a change of use or developments that themselves created contamination risks. Construction of a boundary wall at an existing scrapyard did neither.

The authority was entitled to rely on separate environmental and pollution-control regimes and was not required to duplicate their functions through the planning process.

#### 2. Alleged intensification of use

The claimant's argument that the wall would facilitate a material intensification of scrapyard operations was unsupported by evidence. The application concerned only boundary treatment, and there was no evidential basis for treating it as a mechanism for expanding the established use of the site.

A planning authority is not required to investigate or determine hypothetical future developments unsupported by the material before it.

#### 3. Planning conditions

Although planning authorities may sometimes impose conditions affecting existing uses when granting permission for related development, any such condition must be necessary and directly related to the development permitted.

The court found no basis for imposing a condition restricting storage heights. Such a condition was neither shown to be necessary nor sufficiently connected to the proposed wall, particularly where separate environmental licensing controls already regulated storage heights.

#### 4. Officer reports and reasons

Officer reports must be read realistically and as a whole. Courts will not subject reports to excessive forensic analysis. The report adequately addressed the claimant's objections, explained why conditions were unnecessary, and contained a coherent planning rationale.

#### 5. Green Belt and visual impact

Assessment of visual impact was a matter of planning judgment for the decision-maker. The officer had considered the visibility of the wall, the effect on surrounding land, and relevant planning policies. The conclusion that the new wall would not cause unacceptable visual harm, and might improve the appearance of the site while screening scrapyard operations, was a lawful planning judgment.

### Outcome

The court rejected all grounds of challenge and upheld the grant of planning permission. No error of law was identified in the authority's treatment of contamination, planning history, potential intensification, planning conditions, or Green Belt impacts. The application for judicial review was dismissed.
