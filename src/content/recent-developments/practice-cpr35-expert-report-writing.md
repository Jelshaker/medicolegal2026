## Introduction

A radiological expert report serves the court rather than the instructing party and must clearly outline the material reviewed, the objective imaging findings, assumed facts, professional opinions, and the reasoning behind them. It should identify limitations, note matters requiring another discipline, and remain independent, transparent, and proportionate. Certainty does not strengthen a report; rigorous, evidence-based reasoning within the expert’s competence does. The report belongs to the court process and should avoid duplicating evidence that falls properly within another specialty or providing extensive commentary on matters that do not assist resolution of the pleaded issues.

## The Governing Framework and Overriding Duty

Civil expert evidence in England and Wales is governed by CPR Part 35, Practice Direction 35, the 2014 Expert Guidance, court orders, and professional obligations. Evidence must be reasonably required to resolve the case, and courts typically grant permission only for specific disciplines, issues, or a single joint expert. The expert’s overriding duty is to assist the court on matters within their expertise, a responsibility that supersedes any obligation to the instructing party, the employing organisation, or the fee-payer. This duty applies equally to party-appointed and single joint experts. Expert evidence is not strengthened by appearing certain; it is strengthened by being independent, transparent, reasoned, and strictly confined to the available evidence and court-approved scope.

## Independence and Professional Conduct

Expert evidence must be the independent product of the expert, free from litigation pressure or advocacy. Experts must avoid adopting an adversarial stance, omitting inconvenient evidence, assuming disputed facts without explicit labeling, or tailoring opinions to achieve a desired outcome. They must remain within their current expertise, express only evidence-supported conclusions, and reconsider positions if the evidential basis changes. A balanced report that acknowledges contrary material or explains the reasonable range of professional opinion is more credible than one that avoids complexity or asserts unwarranted certainty. Failure to address material that detracts from the expert’s position is far more likely to undermine the evidence than acknowledging it openly.

## The Single Joint Expert and Communication Protocols

A single joint expert is appointed to advise the court jointly on behalf of multiple parties and owes the same duty to the court as a party-appointed expert. The single joint expert is not a mediator, arbitrator, or advocate, and the court retains responsibility for resolving disputes. Parties should agree joint instructions where possible; if instructions are contested, the expert must seek clarification from all parties, note ambiguities in the report, or seek procedural directions rather than negotiating privately. All substantive communications regarding merits, evidence, or report changes should remain transparent to all instructing parties to avoid any appearance of partiality. When additional material is received, the expert must verify its distribution, record receipt details, and confirm it falls within the instruction, maintaining an accurate audit trail throughout.

## Scoping the Expert Role and Reviewing Material

A high-quality opinion begins by clearly defining the radiological question, distinguishing it from broader clinical, surgical, or legal issues such as treatment success, life expectancy, or ultimate liability. The expert must verify the material reviewed, distinguishing between original DICOM data, screenshots, reports, clinical records, and literature, while explicitly noting missing, illegible, or compressed images. Before interpretation, the expert should confirm patient identifiers, examination dates, modalities, anatomical coverage, and the clinical chronology, ensuring anonymisation does not obscure evidential links. Findings must be clearly separated from assumed facts derived from instructions or records, and expert conclusions must explain the logical chain connecting imaging features to professional opinions. When reviewing images retrospectively, the expert should mitigate hindsight bias by initially examining the original examination before consulting later studies, identifying contemporaneous clinical information, recording unannotated appearances, and clearly distinguishing between direct signs and findings recognised only after knowing the eventual diagnosis.

## Essential Report Structure and Content Requirements

A compliant report must follow the current procedural rules and court order, typically beginning with court details, claim numbers, and the expert’s identity, qualifications, and relevant experience, followed by the mandatory declaration of duty to the court. The instructions section should state who instructed the expert, the date, the precise questions asked, any court-defined issues, and any disputed or agreed assumptions, noting that questions based on erroneous premises should be corrected rather than blindly followed. The material considered should be listed systematically, specifying examination dates, modalities, and whether source DICOM or derived images were supplied. The factual background must summarise only essential information, distinguishing agreed, disputed, instructional, documentary, and known facts to avoid unnecessary repetition.

An imaging chronology should present each study’s date, indication, technique, original findings, contemporaneous report, and relevance to the instructed issues, clearly separating retrospective interpretation from original reporting. Independent imaging findings should be anatomically precise, appropriately measured, linked to source images, and explained in plain language, with annotated figures used only when they are representative, accurately labelled, and supplemented by full text review. The professional standard section should identify the reasonably expected expertise, clinical setting, relevant date, professional guidance, and technical limitations, while avoiding restating the legal test itself.

Breach analysis should address each alleged failure separately, explaining what competent care required, what occurred, why the discrepancy was material, whether a reasonable range of opinion exists, and the final conclusion. The language should avoid definitive legal terms like “negligent,” preferring formulations such as “fell below the standard reasonably expected.” Causation within radiological expertise should identify what a competent report would likely have changed in terms of wording, urgency, imaging, communication, referral, or diagnostic timing, while explicitly stating that questions regarding surgical outcomes, oncological treatment, survival, or non-radiological prognosis fall outside the expert’s competence and require specialist evidence.

Where a reasonable range of professional opinion exists, the report must summarise competing interpretations, the evidence supporting each, points of professional disagreement, the expert’s preferred view, and their degree of confidence, avoiding the presentation of a genuinely contested interpretation as mandatory. Material assumptions must be stated expressly, with alternative outcomes explained where the opinion depends on them. Limitations should describe their specific effect on the conclusion rather than relying on generic disclaimers. The report should identify any missing evidence that could materially alter the opinion, answer each instructed question directly with cross-references to reasoning, and provide a concise summary of conclusions that accurately reflects the detailed analysis without introducing new opinions.

## Compliance, Literature, Measurements, and Presentation

The report must conclude with the current prescribed declarations and statement of truth, ensuring the expert has reviewed the final document, incorporated amendments correctly, verified annexes and figures, confirmed opinions remain current, and omitted no material contrary evidence. Literature and professional guidance relied upon must be relevant to the date in question, accurately represented, placed in clinical context, and distinguished from mandatory legal requirements, noting that compliance or departure from guidance does not automatically determine reasonableness. Radiological measurements should state the sequence used, plane of measurement, anatomical landmarks, reproducibility, calibration, likely variability, and clinical significance, acknowledging that different measurements by other experts may reflect technique or biological ambiguity rather than unreliability.

Figures should improve understanding when each includes the examination date, modality, series or image number, orientation, concise annotation, a caption explaining the relevant feature, and confirmation of derivation from the source examination, with annotations carefully avoiding the implication that a finding was equally conspicuous without them. Advocacy must be avoided by replacing definitive or partisan language with measured expressions such as “in my opinion,” “the images demonstrate,” or “a reasonable range of opinion exists,” while still allowing firm conclusions where the evidence warrants them. Uncertainty should be explicitly stated, with probabilities used only where evidence supports them and numerical percentages avoided when qualitative conclusions are more appropriate.

## Managing Changes, Expert Discussions, and Court Testing

If an opinion changes materially after issuance, the expert must promptly notify instructing parties and the court, explaining what changed, which new evidence prompted it, which conclusions are affected, and whether the earlier report should be supplemented or withdrawn, recognising that updating an opinion for valid evidential reasons aligns with the duty to the court. Written questions under CPR 35.6 should be answered directly, avoiding advocacy, identifying new assumptions, correcting genuine errors, and noting when a question exceeds the original instruction or requires further authority. Expert discussions should focus on identifying agreed and disputed issues, reasons for disagreement, additional material required, and whether differences depend on factual assumptions, with the joint statement accurately recording each expert’s genuine opinion rather than negotiating a compromise dictated by legal representatives.

Oral evidence requires thorough preparation, including rereading the report, reviewing source material, identifying corrections, understanding opposing opinions, locating relevant images, and explaining reasoning in plain language while remaining willing to reconsider propositions and answer questions directly. Common weaknesses in radiological reports include starting with a desired conclusion, treating retrospective visibility as automatic breach, ignoring contemporaneous clinical context, relying on screenshots instead of source imaging, giving opinions outside radiological competence, avoiding the reasonable range of opinion, quoting guidance without analysis, conflating breach with causation, failing to address contrary evidence, and asserting unwarranted certainty.

## Practical Preparation and Conclusion

A functional report structure typically follows: court and claim details, expert identity and qualifications, duty declaration, instructions and issues, material considered, factual background, imaging chronology, independent findings, professional standard, breach analysis, radiological causation, reasonable range of opinion, assumptions and limitations, material capable of changing the opinion, answers to questions, summary, references, declarations, and appendices. Before signing, experts should verify that they have addressed only matters within their competence, complied with court permissions, reviewed the best available imaging, identified relied-upon material, distinguished observations from assumptions, addressed contrary evidence, explained the reasonable range, provided reasoned conclusions, separated breach from causation, identified non-radiological questions, stated limitations, noted changing factors, used current declarations, addressed the report to the court, and would maintain the same opinion if instructed by the opposing party.

A strong Part 35 radiological report is defined not by length or certainty, but by relevant expertise, strict independence, accurate review of source imaging, clear separation of facts and assumptions, reasoned professional opinion, consideration of contrary material, acknowledgment of professional disagreement, careful distinction between breach and causation, transparency about limitations, and full procedural compliance. The single joint expert carries the same overriding duty to the court as a party-appointed expert, with the added requirement of transparent communication with all instructing parties. A report withstands judicial scrutiny when its reasoning is transparent, its limits are candid, and its conclusions follow directly from the evidence rather than the identity of the party who provided the instruction.
